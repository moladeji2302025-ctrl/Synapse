## Q1. Which research systems learn a person's routines or habits from smartphone, computer or web activity?

### Takeaway
Routine learning from phone data is a mature research area (Reality Mining 2006, then MobileMiner, RoutineSense, Banovic's routine models, Sarker's BehavDT and RecencyMiner, routine-change detectors, the StudentLife family of student studies). Nearly all of these systems **measure, model or predict** behaviour for researchers. They seldom **give guidance back to the user**, rarely combine phone **and** computer/web data, and none found combines recency-weighted routine learning, a personal graph and an LLM guide in one consent-gated, user-facing tool. RecencyMiner (2019) comes closest to Synapse's "recent behaviour counts more" idea. Banovic et al. (2016, 2017) come closest to modelling "situation leads to action" routines.

### Cited Findings

**A. Seminal and older systems (all OLDER THAN 10 YEARS; cite as background)**

- **Reality Mining (Eagle & Pentland, 2006).** In plain words, MIT handed phones running logging software to about 100 students and staff and recorded calls, Bluetooth proximity, cell-tower location and phone status for about 9 months. From this they inferred social networks and daily patterns. The aim was to study society, not to help the individual user. This is the classic "your phone can reveal your routine" study. Its venue is *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing*, 10(4), 255–268. The participant count and duration come from my background knowledge and are not verified from the full text. — [doi:10.1007/s00779-005-0046-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00779-005-0046-3)
  - APA: Eagle, N., & Pentland, A. (2006). Reality mining: Sensing complex social systems. *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing, 10*(4), 255–268. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00779-005-0046-3
- **Routine discovery with topic models (Farrahi & Gatica-Perez, 2011).** This work used Reality Mining location data from **97 users over 16 months**. An unsupervised method based on two probabilistic topic models (LDA and the Author-Topic model) found routines such as "going to work late", "going home early" and "working nonstop", and found which routines are typical of particular users or of weekends. It measures only and gives no guidance. — [doi:10.1145/1889681.1889684](https://doi.org/10.1145/1889681.1889684)
  - APA: Farrahi, K., & Gatica-Perez, D. (2011). Discovering routines from large-scale human locations using probabilistic topic models. *ACM Transactions on Intelligent Systems and Technology, 2*(1), Article 3, 1–27. https://doi.org/10.1145/1889681.1889684
- **Social fMRI / Friends and Family study (Aharony et al., 2011).** This was a large MIT phone-sensing study built on the Funf framework. Crossref confirmed the metadata, but no abstract was retrieved, so cite it only as background for rich multi-sensor phone logging. — [doi:10.1016/j.pmcj.2011.09.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmcj.2011.09.004)
  - APA: Aharony, N., Pan, W., Ip, C., Khayal, I., & Pentland, A. (2011). Social fMRI: Investigating and shaping social mechanisms in the real world. *Pervasive and Mobile Computing, 7*(6), 643–659. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmcj.2011.09.004
- **RoutineMaker (Antila et al., 2012, VTT Finland).** This work-in-progress app logged which phone tasks a person did and sent the logs to a server. The server clustered them into candidate "routines", and the user could then turn a routine into an automated task. It was tested in a **two-week study with 10 users**. The authors found that people would automate tasks if the system offered "flexibility and expressiveness without too much information overload". Their stated future work is to refine the algorithms on real-life data and to fix the interaction design. Its logic resembles Synapse's "A then B" suggestions, but it covers the phone only, does not adapt, and is a short workshop paper. — [doi:10.1109/PERCOMW.2012.6197519](https://doi.org/10.1109/PERCOMW.2012.6197519)
  - APA: Antila, V., Polet, J., Lämsä, A., & Liikka, J. (2012). RoutineMaker: Towards end-user automation of daily routines using smartphones. In *2012 IEEE International Conference on Pervasive Computing and Communications Workshops (PERCOM Workshops)* (pp. 399–402). IEEE. https://doi.org/10.1109/PERCOMW.2012.6197519
- **Shin, Hong & Dey (2012), app-usage prediction.** This paper predicts which app a user will open next from phone context (UbiComp 2012, pp. 173–182). It is often cited as the base for app-prediction work. — [doi:10.1145/2370216.2370243](https://doi.org/10.1145/2370216.2370243)
  - APA: Shin, C., Hong, J.-H., & Dey, A. K. (2012). Understanding and prediction of mobile application usage for smart phones. In *Proceedings of the 2012 ACM Conference on Ubiquitous Computing* (pp. 173–182). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2370216.2370243
- **CASAS "smart home in a box" (Cook et al., 2013).** This lightweight sensor kit installs in a home and learns the resident's activities and routines out of the box, with no customisation or training. Its data comes from home ambient sensors, not phones. Synapse can cite it as an example of routine learning from a fixed environment rather than from personal devices. — [doi:10.1109/MC.2012.328](https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.2012.328)
  - APA: Cook, D. J., Crandall, A. S., Thomas, B. L., & Krishnan, N. C. (2013). CASAS: A smart home in a box. *Computer, 46*(7), 62–69. https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.2012.328
- **Where and what (Do & Gatica-Perez, 2014).** This work predicts a user's next location and next app from smartphone data in *Pervasive and Mobile Computing* 12, 79–91. Crossref verified the metadata, but no abstract was retrieved. — [doi:10.1016/j.pmcj.2013.03.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmcj.2013.03.006)
  - APA: Do, T. M. T., & Gatica-Perez, D. (2014). Where and what: Using smartphones to predict next locations and applications in daily life. *Pervasive and Mobile Computing, 12*, 79–91. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmcj.2013.03.006
- **MobileMiner (Srinivasan et al., 2014, Samsung).** This service runs on the phone itself and finds "frequent co-occurrence patterns", meaning context events that tend to happen together (for example, a place plus a time leading to a certain app or call). It was evaluated on data from **106 users over 1–3 months**. The authors argue that mining on the device "provides better privacy guarantees" and less dependence on the cloud, and they show the patterns can improve app-launch and contact UIs. This is the closest older system to Synapse's "A then B" sequences. — [doi:10.1145/2632048.2632052](https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632052)
  - APA: Srinivasan, V., Moghaddam, S., Mukherji, A., Rachuri, K. K., Xu, C., & Tapia, E. M. (2014). MobileMiner: Mining your frequent patterns on your phone. In *Proceedings of the 2014 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing* (pp. 389–400). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632052
- **StudentLife (Wang et al., 2014).** A continuous-sensing Android app followed **48 Dartmouth students for a 10-week term**. It found significant correlations between sensor data and stress, sleep, mood, mental well-being and grades, plus a "term lifecycle" in which stress rises and sleep, activity and conversation fall as workload increases. The dataset is public. It measures only. — [doi:10.1145/2632048.2632054](https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632054)
  - APA: Wang, R., Chen, F., Chen, Z., Li, T., Harari, G., Tignor, S., Zhou, X., Ben-Zeev, D., & Campbell, A. T. (2014). StudentLife: Assessing mental health, academic performance and behavioral trends of college students using smartphones. In *Proceedings of the 2014 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing* (pp. 3–14). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632054
- **SmartGPA (Wang et al., 2015).** This follow-up to StudentLife inferred study duration, partying, class attendance, mobility and conversation from phones during one 10-week term. A lasso linear regression predicted cumulative GPA with **r = 0.81, within ±0.179 of real grades**. The authors say the results "open the way for novel interventions", but the system itself gives no guidance. — [doi:10.1145/2750858.2804251](https://doi.org/10.1145/2750858.2804251)
  - APA: Wang, R., Harari, G., Hao, P., Zhou, X., & Campbell, A. T. (2015). SmartGPA: How smartphones can assess and predict academic performance of college students. In *Proceedings of the 2015 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing* (pp. 295–306). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2750858.2804251
- **RoutineSense (Ranvier et al., 2015, EPFL; MobiQuitous).** This incremental framework builds "complex daily routines" out of simple sensed states such as location and activity. It combines opportunistic sensing with user feedback to find frequent and exceptional routines and lay them out on a timeline. The authors report a "near threefold improvement" over the state of the art on a dataset with rich geographic information. — [doi:10.4108/eai.22-7-2015.2260055](https://doi.org/10.4108/eai.22-7-2015.2260055); [EPFL open PDF](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/208793)
  - APA: Ranvier, J.-E., Catasta, M., Vasirani, M., & Aberer, K. (2015). RoutineSense: A mobile sensing framework for the reconstruction of user routines. In *Proceedings of the 12th EAI International Conference on Mobile and Ubiquitous Systems: Computing, Networking and Services (MOBIQUITOUS 2015)*. ICST. https://doi.org/10.4108/eai.22-7-2015.2260055
- **AWARE framework (Ferreira, Kostakos & Dey, 2015).** This open-source toolkit captures phone context and infers higher-level context from it, with "minimal battery impact". It is useful to cite as the research-grade logging tool that Synapse's Android collector is comparable to. — [doi:10.3389/fict.2015.00006](https://doi.org/10.3389/fict.2015.00006)
  - APA: Ferreira, D., Kostakos, V., & Dey, A. K. (2015). AWARE: Mobile context instrumentation framework. *Frontiers in ICT, 2*, Article 6. https://doi.org/10.3389/fict.2015.00006

**B. Routine and habit learning systems from 2016 onward**

- **Banovic et al. (2016), "Modeling and Understanding Human Routine Behavior" (CHI 2016, pp. 248–260).** In plain words, the paper treats a routine as "in this situation, the person usually does this action". It reuses the by-products of an existing activity-prediction algorithm to capture causal **situation-to-action** links. The method is a Markov decision process with inverse reinforcement learning. The authors tested it on two example datasets and showed that the modelled routines predict people's actions and match findings from earlier research. Their motivation is to let technology "help people improve their bad habits… and other suboptimal routines". The paper models routines but does not deploy guidance to users. — [doi:10.1145/2858036.2858557](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858557); the MDP and IRL method is confirmed by [typeset summary](https://typeset.io/papers/modeling-and-understanding-human-routine-behavior-1fsrwjxcvz)
  - APA: Banovic, N., Buzali, T., Chevalier, F., Mankoff, J., & Dey, A. K. (2016). Modeling and understanding human routine behavior. In *Proceedings of the 2016 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 248–260). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858557
- **Banovic et al. (2017), routine models to detect and generate behaviours (CHI 2017, pp. 6683–6694).** Routines are modelled as "a series of frequent actions that people perform in specific situations". Instead of expensive expert labels, new behaviours are classified by how likely they are under the person's own routine model (weak labelling). The demo system helps drivers notice and understand aggressive driving. The authors frame the work as enabling technology that "can trigger interventions and help people reflect". — [doi:10.1145/3025453.3025571](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025571)
  - APA: Banovic, N., Wang, A., Jin, Y., Chang, C., Ramos, J., Dey, A. K., & Mankoff, J. (2017). Leveraging human routine models to detect and generate human behaviors. In *Proceedings of the 2017 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 6683–6694). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025571
- **Zhao et al. (2016), kinds of smartphone users (UbiComp 2016).** The team analysed **one month of app usage from 106,762 Android users** with two-step clustering and found **382 distinct user types**. They conclude that treating all users as similar harms reproducibility and design. This supports Synapse's per-user (personalised) learning. — [doi:10.1145/2971648.2971696](https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971696)
  - APA: Zhao, S., Ramos, J., Tao, J., Jiang, Z., Li, S., Wu, Z., Pan, G., & Dey, A. K. (2016). Discovering different kinds of smartphone users through their application usage behaviors. In *Proceedings of the 2016 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing* (pp. 498–509). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971696
- **PrefMiner (Mehrotra, Hendley & Musolesi, 2016; UbiComp, pp. 1223–1234).** This system mines a user's notification-handling preferences into rules for managing notifications on the phone. Only the metadata was verified; the abstract was not read. — [doi:10.1145/2971648.2971747](https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971747)
  - APA: Mehrotra, A., Hendley, R., & Musolesi, M. (2016). PrefMiner: Mining user's preferences for intelligent mobile notification management. In *Proceedings of the 2016 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing* (pp. 1223–1234). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971747
- **Mehrotra et al. (2017), places, activities and phone use (IMWUT 1(3)).** This study links where people are and what they are doing to how they use their phone. Only the metadata was verified. — [doi:10.1145/3131901](https://doi.org/10.1145/3131901)
  - APA: Mehrotra, A., Müller, S. R., Harari, G. M., Gosling, S. D., Mascolo, C., Musolesi, M., & Rentfrow, P. J. (2017). Understanding the role of places and activities on mobile phone interaction and usage patterns. *Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies, 1*(3), Article 84, 1–22. https://doi.org/10.1145/3131901
- **TYDR, Track Your Daily Routine (Beierle et al., 2018; MOBILESoft short paper, pp. 72–75).** This Android app tracks phone sensor and usage data and also gives standard personality questionnaires, aiming to predict personality from phone data. It visualises the tracked data for the user. On privacy, it was built to satisfy ethics boards and data-protection officers through **anonymised storage, informing users about collection, and an opt-out option**. It measures only and gives no guidance. — [doi:10.1145/3197231.3197235](https://doi.org/10.1145/3197231.3197235)
  - APA: Beierle, F., Tran, V. T., Allemand, M., Neff, P., Schlee, W., Probst, T., Pryss, R., & Zimmermann, J. (2018). TYDR – Track your daily routine. Android app for tracking smartphone sensor and usage data. In *Proceedings of the 5th International Conference on Mobile Software Engineering and Systems (MOBILESoft '18)* (pp. 72–75). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3197231.3197235
  - Companion journal paper: Beierle et al. (2020) studied **what data smartphone users are willing to share with researchers** using TYDR, and introduced a four-category context data model. The specific percentages were not retrieved (Springer page blocked automated access). — [doi:10.1007/s12652-019-01355-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12652-019-01355-6)
  - APA: Beierle, F., Tran, V. T., Allemand, M., Neff, P., Schlee, W., Probst, T., Zimmermann, J., & Pryss, R. (2020). What data are smartphone users willing to share with researchers? *Journal of Ambient Intelligence and Humanized Computing, 11*(6), 2277–2289. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12652-019-01355-6
- **Sarker's behaviour-rule mining line (Swinburne University).** No paper titled "BehavMiner" could be found in Crossref or a web search, so the name is probably informal. Cite the verified papers below instead. — [search result summary](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1804.01379)
  - **Association-rule mining of behaviour (Sarker & Salim, 2018; PAKDD, LNCS, pp. 450–461).** This method extracts a concise, non-redundant set of behavioural association rules (context leads to phone action) from smartphone logs. — [doi:10.1007/978-3-319-93034-3_36](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-93034-3_36)
    - APA: Sarker, I. H., & Salim, F. D. (2018). Mining user behavioral rules from smartphone data through association analysis. In D. Phung et al. Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-93034-3_36 (volume number from my background knowledge, not verified)
  - **Individualised time-series segmentation (Sarker et al., 2018; *The Computer Journal* 61(3), 349–368).** This method splits each user's day into time segments that fit that user's own behaviour, instead of fixed hours. That idea maps onto Synapse's per-user "time" nodes. — [doi:10.1093/comjnl/bxx082](https://doi.org/10.1093/comjnl/bxx082)
    - APA: Sarker, I. H., Colman, A., Kabir, M. A., & Han, J. (2018). Individualized time-series segmentation for mining mobile phone user behavior. *The Computer Journal, 61*(3), 349–368. https://doi.org/10.1093/comjnl/bxx082
  - **RecencyMiner (Sarker, Colman & Han, 2019; *Journal of Big Data* 6, Art. 49). This is the closest match to Synapse's adaptive weighting.** The authors argue that phone behaviour "may not be static… changing over time", so "an individual's recent behavioral patterns… are more likely to be interesting and significant than older ones". RecencyMiner mines **recency-based personalised behaviour rules** and, on real individual datasets, predicted phone use better than baseline models by lowering error rates. It is phone only and offline; it gives no user-facing guidance, no LLM and no cross-device data. — [doi:10.1186/s40537-019-0211-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0211-6)
    - APA: Sarker, I. H., Colman, A., & Han, J. (2019). RecencyMiner: Mining recency-based personalized behavior from contextual smartphone data. *Journal of Big Data, 6*, Article 49. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0211-6
  - **BehavDT (Sarker et al., 2020; *Mobile Networks and Applications* 25(3), 1151–1161).** This is a "behavioral decision tree" that outputs general decisions plus context-specific exceptions, to avoid the over-fitting of ordinary decision trees. On individual users' real phone datasets it beat traditional machine-learning models. — [doi:10.1007/s11036-019-01443-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11036-019-01443-z)
    - APA: Sarker, I. H., Colman, A., Han, J., Khan, A. I., Abushark, Y. B., & Salah, K. (2020). BehavDT: A behavioral decision tree learning to build user-centric context-aware predictive model. *Mobile Networks and Applications, 25*(3), 1151–1161. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11036-019-01443-z
  - Sarker, Kayes & Watters (2019) compared machine-learning classifiers for predicting personalised, context-aware phone use. — [doi:10.1186/s40537-019-0219-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0219-y)
    - APA: Sarker, I. H., Kayes, A. S. M., & Watters, P. (2019). Effectiveness analysis of machine learning classification models for predicting personalized context-aware smartphone usage. *Journal of Big Data, 6*, Article 57. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0219-y
- **Pielot et al. (2017), "Beyond Interruptibility" (IMWUT 1(3)).** In a field study, **337 users ran an app for 4 weeks**, producing 120 million phone-use events and 78,930 notifications. A model predicted whether a user would engage with offered content. It had a **66.6% higher success rate than a naive baseline, and 5× better when past behaviour was included**. This supports timing Synapse's suggestions to "opportune moments". — [doi:10.1145/3130956](https://doi.org/10.1145/3130956)
  - APA: Pielot, M., Cardoso, B., Katevas, K., Serrà, J., Matic, A., & Oliver, N. (2017). Beyond interruptibility: Predicting opportune moments to engage mobile phone users. *Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies, 1*(3), Article 91, 1–25. https://doi.org/10.1145/3130956
- **Xu et al. (2019), routine behaviour features for depression detection among college students (IMWUT 3(3)).** Association-rule mining over passively sensed phone and wearable data yields interpretable "behaviour rules", which are fed to a classifier. The model beat unimodal features by **9.7% on average** and generalised to a second dataset. It is a measurement tool for clinicians and researchers, not for user guidance. — [doi:10.1145/3351274](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351274)
  - APA: Xu, X., Chikersal, P., Doryab, A., Villalba, D. K., Dutcher, J. M., Tumminia, M. J., Althoff, T., Cohen, S., Creswell, K. G., Creswell, J. D., Mankoff, J., & Dey, A. K. (2019). Leveraging routine behavior and contextually-filtered features for depression detection among college students. *Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies, 3*(3), Article 116, 1–33. https://doi.org/10.1145/3351274
- **Wang et al. (2018), depression dynamics in college students (IMWUT 2(1)).** **83 Dartmouth students** were followed over two 9-week terms. Phone and wearable "symptom features" predicted weekly depression with **81.5% recall and 69.1% precision**. — [doi:10.1145/3191775](https://doi.org/10.1145/3191775)
  - APA: Wang, R., Wang, W., daSilva, A., Huckins, J. F., Kelley, W. M., Heatherton, T. F., & Campbell, A. T. (2018). Tracking depression dynamics in college students using mobile phone and wearable sensing. *Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies, 2*(1), Article 43, 1–26. https://doi.org/10.1145/3191775
- **Routine-change detection (Koch, Kastanos & Aharonson, 2020; IEEE ICHMS).** A phone app recorded multi-modal sensor data from **60 users**. A multi-task MLP classified the 14 most common activities with **65.7% accuracy**. Four anomaly detectors were compared, using 2 weeks of training data, and a **one-class SVM detected abnormal routine changes with 76.8% accuracy**. The authors call these results "preliminary". This directly shows that routine **change** can be detected, which Synapse handles instead by adapting through recency weighting. — [doi:10.1109/ICHMS49158.2020.9209417](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHMS49158.2020.9209417)
  - APA: Koch, A. de M., Kastanos, N., & Aharonson, V. (2020). Monitoring routine changes from daily smartphone usage. In *2020 IEEE International Conference on Human-Machine Systems (ICHMS)* (pp. 1–6). IEEE. https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHMS49158.2020.9209417
- **GLOBEM dataset (Xu et al., 2022; NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks).** This is a multi-year passive-sensing dataset for testing whether behaviour models still work across years and people, i.e. longitudinal generalisation. Only the metadata was verified; no abstract was retrieved. — [doi:10.52202/068431-1790](https://doi.org/10.52202/068431-1790)
  - APA: Xu, X., Zhang, H., Sefidgar, Y., Ren, Y., Liu, X., Seo, W., Brown, J., Kuehn, K., Merrill, M., Nurius, P., Patel, S., Althoff, T., Morris, M. E., Riskin, E., Mankoff, J., & Dey, A. K. - **College Experience Study (Nepal et al., 2024; IMWUT 8(1)).** **Over 200 Dartmouth students were tracked for four years**, "the longest longitudinal mobile sensing study to date", through COVID-19. It found persistent behaviour patterns and that some behaviours returned to normal while others stayed elevated. The authors report that **frequent phone OS updates made long-term sensing, data completeness and compliance hard**, which is a practical warning for Synapse's Android collector. — [doi:10.1145/3643501](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643501)
  - APA: Nepal, S., Liu, W., Pillai, A., Wang, W., Vojdanovski, V., Huckins, J. F., Rogers, C., Meyer, M. L., & Campbell, A. T. (2024). - **DiversityOne (Busso et al., 2025; IMWUT).** This dataset covers **782 college students in eight countries** (China, Denmark, India, Italy, Mexico, Mongolia, Paraguay, UK) over four weeks, with 26 sensor types and 350K+ self-reports. The authors note that most existing datasets are "focusing on specific countries primarily in the Global North". **No African country is included.** — [doi:10.1145/3712289](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712289)
  - APA: Busso, M., Bontempelli, A., Malcotti, L. J., Meegahapola, L., Kun, P., Diwakar, S., Nutakki, C., Britez, M. R., et al. (2025). - **SHIRBT-MMF (Lee et al., 2025; IMWUT 9(4)).** This framework mines **fine-grained "routine tasks" from in-app UI state transitions** using multi-level sequential pattern mining, and uses **an LLM to model the tasks automatically**. It then predicts daily stress. On **26 participants (1- and 4-month datasets)** the personalised model reached **75% accuracy, 5% above baselines, using only 3–6% of app types**, and its features stayed stable over time. The authors explicitly criticise app-usage-statistics approaches as "susceptible to distribution shift and feature evolution". It is the most recent and closest example of an LLM plus routine sequence mining, but it measures stress rather than giving guidance. — [doi:10.1145/3770644](https://doi.org/10.1145/3770644)
  - APA: Lee, H., Park, T., Koh, Y., Lee, J., & Lee, U. (2025). **C. Computer and web activity tracking (Synapse's web side)**

### Inferences
- Nearly every routine-learning system above **measures or predicts** for researchers (StudentLife, SmartGPA, Xu 2019, SHIRBT, DiversityOne) or is offline algorithm work (Sarker's line, Banovic 2016). Only RoutineMaker turned learned routines into something the user acts on, and it was a 10-person, two-week workshop prototype.
- Only RecencyMiner explicitly **weights recent behaviour more**. SHIRBT and GLOBEM treat change over time as a robustness problem for the model; Koch et al. treat it as anomaly detection. So "adapting to habit change" is recognised as open, and Synapse can claim a user-facing, recency-weighted approach.
- **Phone and computer/web together** appears only in Rooksby et al. (2016), as screen-time totals, with no learning. Phone-side and PC-side research are otherwise separate literatures. This is a clear gap Synapse can claim.
- No routine system found stores what it learns as a **personal knowledge graph** of apps, places, times and tasks. Balog and colleagues' PKG work (see Q2) is conceptual, so Synapse's graph is a novel combination rather than a novel idea.

## Q2. Which research prototypes are personal/proactive assistants or time-management and study-planning tools (including LLM assistants with long-term memory)?

### Takeaway
The proactive-assistant literature ranges from pre-LLM intent prediction (Cortana-style KP2, CALO/PExA) and human-in-the-loop agents (Calendar.help) to chatbot interventions (SwitchBot, UpTime, Reflection Companion). Since 2023 there are LLM systems with memory (MemoryBank, CareCall long-term memory, Memoro) and LLMs that interpret sensing data (Englhardt 2024, GLOSS 2025, ContextAgent 2025). None of them learns routines from the user's own cross-device app and web logs and then guides a student through the day. The student time-management tools (SortOut, meTime, TimeAware) rely on manual input or simple tracking, not learned routines.

### Inferences
- Pre-LLM proactive assistants (KP2, Benetka) learn from context but were evaluated offline on logs. The deployed chatbot interventions (SwitchBot, UpTime, Reflection Companion) give real guidance but use **hand-written dialogue rules**, not learned routines.
- LLM work since 2023 either adds **memory of conversations** (MemoryBank, CareCall, Generative Agents) or **interprets sensing data for clinicians or researchers** (Englhardt, GLOSS). ContextAgent is proactive but uses wearable video and audio and a benchmark. None found grounds an LLM guide in a **user-owned, consent-gated, cross-device routine graph**, and none is aimed at students' daily planning.
- Student-specific guidance tools (SortOut, meTime's student deployment) show positive effects in 4-week to term-long studies, but they depend on manual task entry or raw awareness. This leaves room for Synapse's automatically learned routines.
- Meurisch (2020), Jo (2024) and Tseng (2019) all point to **user control over proactivity and memory** as a design requirement. This matches Synapse's per-suggestion confirmation and consent categories.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found evaluates an LLM assistant grounded in a user's own smartphone **and** web activity logs in a field deployment. The closest items are preprints or benchmark-only.
- "Proactive Agent" (Lu et al., ICLR 2025) was suggested by background knowledge but not verified this session, so it is not cited.
- No peer-reviewed study-planning or timetable-optimisation system for university students that learns from device data was found.

## Q4. What limitations do the authors themselves report, and which does Synapse address?

### Takeaway
The authors' own stated limitations cluster into five themes that line up with Synapse's design: (1) behaviour changes over time, (2) data alone does not change behaviour, so actionable guidance is needed, (3) people want control over proactivity and memory, (4) collection is narrow (one device or a Global North population), and (5) long-term sensing is fragile.

### Cited Findings
- **Behaviour is not static.** "An individual's phone usage behavior may not be static… changing over time", which is why RecencyMiner was built — [RecencyMiner](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0211-6). App-usage features are "susceptible to distribution shift and feature evolution" — [SHIRBT-MMF](https://doi.org/10.1145/3770644). Koch et al. describe their routine-change results as only "preliminary" — [Koch 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHMS49158.2020.9209417). **Synapse addresses this** with recency-weighted routine learning.
- **Data access is not enough; people need actionable guidance.** Most tools carry "an implicit assumption that providing access to self-tracking data is sufficient" — [Reflection Companion](https://doi.org/10.1145/3214273). "Actionable insights… are likely needed to foster productive behavior change" — [WorkAnalytics](https://doi.org/10.1145/3134714). meTime did not increase time in productivity apps — [meTime](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858193). Good Vibrations' 20% reduction "did not persist after the intervention was removed" — [Okeke 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229463). **Synapse addresses this** with context-aware suggestions and an LLM guide. It should still test whether its effects persist.
- **Control, timing and overload.** RoutineMaker users wanted flexibility "without too much information overload" — [RoutineMaker](https://doi.org/10.1109/PERCOMW.2012.6197519). UpTime warns against "overloading users' existing communication channels" — [UpTime](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300697). Expectations of proactivity vary by person and context — [Meurisch 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3432193). Long-term memory raised privacy concerns, and designers must decide "what topics need to be remembered" — [Jo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642420). **Synapse addresses this** with per-category consent, per-suggestion confirmation, and user export/delete.
- **Narrow data sources.** Earlier proactive agents rely on "enclosed environments (e.g., desktop UIs)" or rules — [ContextAgent](https://doi.org/10.52202/085713-5582). Users wanted per-device and per-app views across devices — [Rooksby 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858055). Datasets focus "primarily in the Global North" — [DiversityOne](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712289). Existing datasets "overlook the unique socioeconomic and infrastructural contexts of African institutions" — [MakOne](https://doi.org/10.1145/3714394.3756183). **Synapse addresses this** with phone and web collection in one per-user store, and a Nigerian student case study.
- **Long-term sensing is fragile.** "Frequent phone OS updates pose significant challenges for mobile sensing apps, data completeness and compliance" — [Nepal 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643501). **Synapse does not fully solve this.** It is a risk to acknowledge (Android background limits).
- **One user model does not fit all.** 382 distinct user types were found among 106,762 users — [Zhao 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971696). **Synapse addresses this** with per-user models and a per-user graph.

### Inferences
- A defensible gap statement: *"Prior systems either learn routines without guiding the user (StudentLife, Banovic, Sarker), guide the user without learning routines (SortOut, SwitchBot, UpTime), or track several devices without learning (Rooksby). LLM assistants with memory remember conversations rather than behaviour. Synapse combines consent-gated phone and web logging, recency-weighted routine and sequence learning stored as a personal graph, and an LLM guide, evaluated with Nigerian university students, a population absent from existing datasets."*
- Synapse does **not** address OS-update fragility, the persistence of behaviour change, or rigorous clinical validation. It should list these as its own limitations.

### Gaps
- Limitations here are taken from abstracts. Full "Limitations" sections were not read for most papers.

## Q5. Are there any systems built or studied in Nigeria or Africa?

### Takeaway
Very little exists. One refereed African passive-sensing student dataset was found (MakOne, Makerere University, Uganda, UbiComp 2025 Companion). There are older phone-metadata studies in Rwanda (Blumenstock 2015) and Nigerian-authored work done in the US (Okeke 2018). Nigerian work on student phone use is descriptive surveys, with no machine-learning routine systems found. This absence is itself a citable gap for Synapse.

### Cited Findings
- **MakOne (Kizito et al., 2025; UbiComp/ISWC '25 Companion).** A six-week multimodal dataset from **72 Makerere University students** in Kampala, Uganda, collected with the iLog sensing app. It covers location, physical activity and screen usage plus EMAs on mood and **daily routines**. The motivation: "most existing behavioural datasets originate from Western institutions and overlook the unique socioeconomic and infrastructural contexts of African institutions." It is a companion-proceedings (dataset/short) paper, refereed but lighter than a full paper; an arXiv version also exists. — [doi:10.1145/3714394.3756183](https://doi.org/10.1145/3714394.3756183); [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22334v1)
  - APA: Kizito, M., Kayongo, ?., Nyende, ?., Chongomweru, H., Muyama, L., Asiku, ?., & Mugisha, ?. (2025). MakOne: Behavioural data of university students' smart devices in Uganda. In *Companion of the 2025 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing* (UbiComp Companion '25). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3714394.3756183 (several first initials and the first author's given name were not retrieved; the "?" placeholders must be filled from the DOI page before use)
- **Blumenstock, Cadamuro & On (2015), Rwanda. OLDER THAN 10 YEARS.** Mobile-phone metadata predicted poverty and wealth (*Science* 350(6264), 1073–1076). This is an African phone-data behaviour model, at population level rather than personal routines. — [doi:10.1126/science.aac4420](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4420)
  - APA: Blumenstock, J., Cadamuro, G., & On, R. (2015). Predicting poverty and wealth from mobile phone metadata. *Science, 350*(6264), 1073–1076. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4420
- **Eagle, Quinn & Clauset (2009). OLDER THAN 10 YEARS.** Methods for continuous cell-tower data from **215 randomly sampled subjects in a major urban city**, including an "X-Factor" dynamic Bayesian network for abnormal behaviour (Pervasive 2009, LNCS). A web result mentions a "Reality Mining Africa" comparison of University of Nairobi and MIT students. Whether this paper's city is in Kenya was **not confirmed**. — [doi:10.1007/978-3-642-01516-8_23](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-01516-8_23); [aitopics tag page](https://aitopics.org/tag/kenyan%20user)
- **Okeke et al. (2018).** The first author, Fabian Okeke, is a Nigerian-origin HCI researcher, but the study used US MTurk participants. Cite it for the intervention, not as African work. — [doi:10.1145/3229434.3229463](https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229463)
- **Nigerian student phone-use studies are descriptive surveys, not systems.**
  - Etim & Olatokun (2024, IGI Global chapter) studied app use for learning at two federal universities. — [IGI](https://igi-global.com/chapter/the-use-of-mobile-technology-tools-and-apps-to-support-students-learning-goals/333731)
  - Elegbe & Dada surveyed 500 University of Ibadan undergraduates. — [UI repository](https://repository.ui.edu.ng/items/a45de402-accf-4011-a572-b7a7a2687d6f/full)
  - CcHub's 2014 survey of over 5,000 mostly University of Lagos students is industry work, not peer-reviewed. — [TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2014/12/19/cchub-publishes-new-report-students-use-phones/)
- **DiversityOne's eight countries include no African country**, which confirms the regional gap. — [doi:10.1145/3712289](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712289)

### Inferences
- Synapse would be among the first routine-learning, guidance-giving systems evaluated with Nigerian university students. MakOne (Uganda) is the nearest regional precedent and should be cited as evidence that African student behaviour data is newly emerging.
- Nigerian-context factors that the MakOne authors flag (infrastructure, socioeconomics) suggest Synapse's evaluation should report data-cost and connectivity effects on syncing. This is my inference, not stated by any source.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Nigerian machine-learning or passive-sensing routine study was found via Crossref or web search. The Etim & Olatokun and Elegbe & Dada items were not DOI-verified and are not core citations.
- Whether Eagle et al. (2009) used Kenyan data was not confirmed from the full text.
- African Journals Online (AJOL) and Nigerian conference proceedings (e.g., the Nigeria Computer Society) were not searched; a manual check there is recommended.
