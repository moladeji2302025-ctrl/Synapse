# Synapse Unites Routine Learning, Consent and Guidance

Synapse is not the first system to learn routines from a phone. It is not the first assistant to make suggestions either. Its defensible contribution is the **combination**: consent-gated logging of both Android phone and web activity, routine learning that gives recent behaviour more weight so the model adapts as habits change, and a guide built on a large language model (LLM, an AI system trained on huge amounts of text that reads and writes natural language), aimed at university students. Across more than 30 research systems, the ones that learn routines (StudentLife, Banovic's routine models, Sarker's RecencyMiner) only measure or predict. The ones that guide (SortOut, SwitchBot, UpTime) do not learn routines from device data. Only one tracked phone and computer together (Rooksby et al., 2016), and it learned nothing. Commercial products split the same way. Samsung and Apple learn routines only inside their own hardware. Trackers measure. Calendar AIs schedule what users type in. Microsoft Recall captures everything with one on/off switch. For the AI models, **privacy terms decide the choice more than quality does**. Gemini's free tier trains on what you send and tells developers not to submit personal information. So the defensible free setup is Groq's `gpt-oss-120b` with zero data retention, or Cloudflare Workers AI's Gemma 4, for the text guide, and Gemma 4 for the vision companion (on Cloudflare, or on the phone itself). For data, **LSApp** is the best public dataset to study (292 users, about 600,000 events, open on GitHub), with the Tsinghua App Usage Dataset as a second test. For algorithms, slot-based periodic mining, PrefixSpan sequence mining, the lift measure, exponential decay and a Hebbian-weighted graph are transparent, cheap, and work from a few weeks of data, which deep-learning models do not. Two cautions hold throughout. Many classic algorithm references come from background knowledge and need their DOIs checked. And the gap must be framed as **cross-platform, consent-first, adaptive and student-focused**, because Apple and Google are moving towards proactive help inside their own ecosystems.

> **How to read the labels in this report.** A DOI (Digital Object Identifier) is the permanent link to a paper. **[K]** means the reference came from the researchers' background knowledge and its DOI was not checked online; check it at doi.org before citing. **[V]** means the details were confirmed online. **(older than 10 years)** marks work published before 2016; the department prefers 2016 onward, so use these only as background. **(preprint)** marks work not yet peer-reviewed; do not count it as a source. Every worked example uses **made-up numbers**, chosen to teach the method; the arithmetic has been checked so you can repeat it aloud.

## Research systems learn routines or guide users, but rarely both

The research question for the problem-statement panellist is simple: has anyone already built Synapse? The answer from the literature is "pieces of it, never the whole". The work falls into five strands. Each strand covers one or two of Synapse's parts and leaves out the rest.

### Seminal studies proved that phones reveal routines (all older than 10 years)

The founding study is **Reality Mining** at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). Phones running logging software recorded calls, Bluetooth proximity, cell-tower location and phone status for about 100 students and staff over about nine months, and the researchers inferred social networks and daily patterns ([Eagle & Pentland, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00779-005-0046-3)). The participant count and duration come from background knowledge and should be checked. Its purpose was to study society, not to help the user. Farrahi and Gatica-Perez then ran two topic models (latent Dirichlet allocation, or LDA, and the Author-Topic model, both of which find hidden "themes" in data) over **97 users' location data for 16 months**. They discovered routines such as "going to work late" and "working nonstop" ([Farrahi & Gatica-Perez, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1889681.1889684)). It measured and gave no guidance.

Three older systems sit closest to Synapse's logic. **RoutineMaker** clustered phone task logs into candidate routines that users could turn into automations. It was tested on **10 users for two weeks**, and users wanted "flexibility and expressiveness without too much information overload" ([Antila et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1109/PERCOMW.2012.6197519)). **MobileMiner** from Samsung found "frequent co-occurrence patterns" (context events that tend to happen together, such as a place plus a time leading to an app) on the phone itself, across **106 users over 1–3 months**. Its authors argued that mining on the device "provides better privacy guarantees" ([Srinivasan et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632052)). **StudentLife** followed **48 Dartmouth students for a 10-week term** with a sensing app. It found links between phone data and stress, sleep, mood and grades ([Wang et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632054)). Its follow-up, SmartGPA, predicted grade point average (GPA) with **r = 0.81** using lasso regression (a linear model that drops unhelpful inputs) ([Wang et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2750858.2804251)). The authors said this "opens the way for novel interventions", but built none. Other background systems include RoutineSense, which builds daily routines from simple sensed states with user feedback ([Ranvier et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.4108/eai.22-7-2015.2260055)); the AWARE open-source logging toolkit, which is the research-grade equivalent of Synapse's Android collector ([Ferreira et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fict.2015.00006)); next-app prediction from phone context ([Shin, Hong & Dey, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1145/2370216.2370243)); and the CASAS "smart home in a box", which learns routines from home sensors rather than personal devices ([Cook et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.2012.328)).

### Since 2016, routine learning became personal and recency-aware

Newer work sharpened the idea of a routine. Banovic and colleagues define a routine as "in this situation, the person usually does this action". They model it with a Markov decision process (MDP, a model of states, actions and the rewards that drive choices) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL, which works backwards from observed choices to the goals behind them) ([Banovic et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858557)). A follow-up used each person's own routine model to flag unusual behaviour such as aggressive driving, and framed the work as technology that "can trigger interventions" ([Banovic et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025571)). Neither deployed guidance to real users. Zhao and colleagues clustered **one month of app use from 106,762 Android users** and found **382 distinct user types**. That is strong evidence for Synapse's per-user models rather than one model for everyone ([Zhao et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971696)).

The closest academic relative of Synapse's adaptive weighting is Iqbal Sarker's line of work at Swinburne University. **Correction to the research notes:** BehavMiner does exist as a peer-reviewed workshop paper, Sarker, I. H. (2018), *BehavMiner: Mining user behaviors from mobile phone data for personalized services*, in the 2018 Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) International Conference on Pervasive Computing and Communications Workshops, pp. 452–453 ([Sarker, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/PERCOMW.2018.8480325)). It is a two-page workshop paper, so it is best cited alongside the fuller papers in the same line. Sarker and Salim mined concise association rules (context leads to phone action) from smartphone logs ([Sarker & Salim, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-93034-3_36)). Sarker and colleagues split each user's day into time segments that fit that user, instead of fixed hours, which maps onto Synapse's per-user time nodes ([Sarker et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/comjnl/bxx082)). **RecencyMiner** is the key one. Its authors argue that phone behaviour "may not be static… changing over time", so "recent behavioral patterns… are more likely to be interesting and significant than older ones". It mines recency-based rules and predicted phone use with lower error than baselines ([Sarker, Colman & Han, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-019-0211-6)). It is phone-only and offline, with no user-facing guidance, no LLM and no cross-device data. BehavDT, a "behavioural decision tree" that adds context-specific exceptions to general rules, beat traditional classifiers on individual users' data ([Sarker et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11036-019-01443-z)).

Change over time is now recognised as an open problem. Koch and colleagues detected abnormal routine changes in **60 users** with a one-class support vector machine (SVM, a model that learns what "normal" looks like and flags the rest) at **76.8% accuracy**, which they call "preliminary" ([Koch et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHMS49158.2020.9209417)). The most recent close relative is **SHIRBT-MMF**, which mines fine-grained "routine tasks" from in-app screen transitions, uses an LLM to model them, and predicts daily stress at **75% accuracy in 26 participants**. Its authors warn that app-usage statistics are "susceptible to distribution shift and feature evolution" ([Lee et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3770644)). It measures stress; it does not guide. Student-focused sensing keeps growing too. Phone and wearable features predicted weekly depression in 83 students ([Wang et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3191775)). Association-rule "behaviour rules" improved depression detection by **9.7%** ([Xu et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351274)). The College Experience Study tracked **over 200 Dartmouth students for four years** and warned that "frequent phone OS updates pose significant challenges" for long-term sensing ([Nepal et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3643501)). That is a practical warning for Synapse's Android collector. Pielot and colleagues showed that timing matters: in **337 users over 4 weeks**, predicting "opportune moments" was **66.6% better than a naive baseline, and 5× better when past behaviour was included** ([Pielot et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3130956)).

### Phone research and computer research grew up separately

On the computer side, tools measure but rarely learn. TimeAware showed PC usage to **24 people for 8 weeks**, and only those shown negative framing (stressing distractions) improved ([Kim et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858428)). meTime cut social media and browsing across **118 participants, including students**, but did not increase time in productive apps ([Whittaker et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858193)). WorkAnalytics concluded that **"actionable insights"** are needed for behaviour change; raw tracking is not enough ([Meyer et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3134714)). The single cross-device precedent is **Personal Tracking of Screen Time**, which combined screen time from iOS, Android, Mac and Windows for **21 users over 28+ days**. Users cared more about specific devices and apps than about total screen time ([Rooksby et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858055)). It did self-tracking only, with no routine learning and no suggestions. That leaves phone-plus-web routine learning as a clear opening.

### Assistants and LLM memory guide people without learning their behaviour

Before LLMs, proactive assistants predicted needs from context but were tested offline on logs. KP2 predicted what a Cortana-style assistant user wanted right now ([Sun et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2939672.2939676)). Benetka and colleagues predicted a user's next activity and showed matching information cards ([Benetka et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3018661.3018679)). The deployed chatbots did guide people, but from hand-written scripts. SwitchBot helped **34 workers over 14 days** detach from and reattach to work ([Williams et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173662)). UpTime blocked distracting sites after breaks for **15 workers over 3 weeks**, and warned against "overloading users' existing communication channels" ([Tseng et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300697)). Reflection Companion ran daily mini-dialogues about Fitbit data, and **16 of 33 users kept using it unpaid**. Its authors criticised the "implicit assumption that providing access to self-tracking data is sufficient" ([Kocielnik et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3214273)). A study of **272 people** found that how much initiative people want from an assistant varies by person and context, so proactivity should be adjustable ([Meurisch et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3432193)). That directly supports Synapse's per-suggestion confirmation.

The closest student tool is **SortOut**, a persuasive time-management app. In a **4-week study with 34 students**, it improved time management and academic confidence and lowered stress ([Alhasani & Orji, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2023.2297330)). But students type their tasks in; it learns nothing from device data. Phone-limit nudges show the same pattern: Good Vibrations cut daily use by **over 20%**, but the effect **did not last** once removed ([Okeke et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229463)).

Since 2023, LLM systems have added memory, but memory of **conversations**, not behaviour. Generative Agents retrieve memories by recency, importance and relevance ([Park et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3586183.3606763)). MemoryBank lets memories fade on a forgetting curve, which is close to Synapse's recency idea, applied to chat ([Zhong et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v38i17.29946)). A health chatbot with long-term memory increased disclosure but raised **privacy concerns**; its authors advise "carefully deciding what topics need to be remembered" ([Jo et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642420)). LLMs reading sensing data proved most useful for **plain-language summaries** rather than diagnosis ([Englhardt et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3659604)). Narrated step data caused more reflection than charts alone in **273 people** ([Strömel et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642032)). GLOSS, a group of LLMs making sense of phone and wearable data, reached **87.93% accuracy against 29.31%** for standard retrieval-augmented generation (RAG, where the model is fed retrieved records before answering) ([Choube et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3749474)). ContextAgent calls itself "the first context-aware proactive agent", using wearable video and audio, but it was tested on a benchmark, not with real users ([Yang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.52202/085713-5582)). Two related preprints (EMG-RAG and a 7-person memory-chatbot pilot) exist but are **preprints** and do not count ([arXiv 2409.19401](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.19401.pdf); [arXiv 2510.07925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07925)). Synapse's graph also has a name in the literature: a personal knowledge graph (PKG), meaning structured information about things "personally related to the user" ([Balog & Kenter, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3341981.3344241)), owned by one individual for personalised services ([Skjæveland et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aiopen.2024.01.003)). That work is conceptual, so Synapse's graph is a new combination, not a new idea.

### Africa barely appears in the data

The regional gap is real and citable. DiversityOne covers **782 students in eight countries, none of them African**, and its authors note datasets focus "primarily in the Global North" ([Busso et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712289)). The only African student sensing dataset found is **MakOne**: **72 Makerere University students** in Uganda over six weeks, including survey answers about daily routines. Its authors say existing datasets "overlook the unique socioeconomic and infrastructural contexts of African institutions" ([Kizito et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3714394.3756183)). It is a short companion-proceedings paper, and several author initials still need filling in from the DOI page. Older African phone work is population-level, such as predicting poverty from Rwandan phone metadata ([Blumenstock et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4420), older than 10 years). Nigerian studies of student phone use found so far are descriptive surveys, not learning systems ([Elegbe & Dada, University of Ibadan repository](https://repository.ui.edu.ng/items/a45de402-accf-4011-a572-b7a7a2687d6f/full)). African Journals Online and Nigeria Computer Society proceedings were not searched, so check them by hand before claiming "first".

A defensible gap statement follows from the table. Prior systems either learn routines without guiding the user (StudentLife, Banovic, Sarker), guide the user without learning routines (SortOut, SwitchBot, UpTime), or track several devices without learning (Rooksby). LLM assistants with memory remember conversations rather than behaviour. Synapse combines consent-gated phone and web logging, recency-weighted routine and sequence learning stored as a personal graph, and an LLM guide, evaluated with Nigerian university students, a group absent from existing datasets. Be honest about what Synapse does not solve: Android update fragility, whether behaviour change lasts, and clinical validity. Also note one limit of this review: consent mechanisms were judged from abstracts, so read the full texts of RecencyMiner, Banovic 2016, Rooksby 2016, SHIRBT-MMF and TYDR before writing "no prior system offers per-category consent". TYDR, for instance, already used anonymised storage, user information and an opt-out ([Beierle et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3197231.3197235)).

## Commercial products each cover at most two of Synapse's six pieces

Commercial tools fall into five groups. None learns one routine model from both phone app use and web browsing across vendors, under per-category consent, and then guides a student. Every item below was checked against sources accessed on 13 November 2026, but many 2026 facts come from tech press, not vendor pages.

| Group | Examples | What it does | Learns routines automatically? | Status, November 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Platform assistants | Gemini (Android), Siri, Samsung Modes and Routines | Voice and app help; some suggestions | Partly, inside one vendor only | Google Assistant retired on Android phones from 4 September 2026 |
| Usage trackers | Digital Wellbeing, Screen Time, RescueTime | Measure and limit app and site use | No; category labels only | Active |
| AI calendars | Reclaim.ai, Motion | Place user-entered tasks and habits on a calendar | No; adapt to declared habits | Clockwise shut down 27 March 2026 |
| Total-recall memory | Microsoft Recall | Screenshot everything for later search | No | Rewind/Limitless shut down December 2025 |
| Screen-aware agents | Copilot Vision, Siri onscreen awareness, Claude/Gemini/OpenAI agents | Help with what is on screen now | No long-term model | Several US-first; tiers disputed |

Only two products clearly learn routines on their own. Samsung's Modes and Routines offers AI "auto suggestions" generated from usage patterns in Galaxy's Personal Data Core, based on time, place and occasion, but only on Galaxy devices ([Samsung Support](https://www.samsung.com/sa_en/support/mobile-devices/How-can-AI-affect-Modes-and-Routines)). Apple's Siri Suggestions learns on-device which app a user opens at a given time or place (background knowledge, not re-verified). Apple released "Siri AI" in September 2026 with onscreen awareness and personal context ([Apple Newsroom, Sep 2026](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/siri-ai-a-profoundly-more-capable-and-personal-assistant-is-here/)). On Android, Gemini replaced Google Assistant as the default on phones, and users cannot switch back ([Android Headlines](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2026/09/google-assistant-killed-android-gemini.html); [Implicator](https://www.implicator.ai/google-sets-september-4-date-to-replace-assistant-with-gemini-on-android/)). Reports conflict on whether Gemini supports routines yet. Google's May 2026 "proactive briefs" were reported second-hand only ([Renascence summary](https://www.renascence.io/news/15573/google-assistant-end-of-life-september-2026-gemini-takes-over)). The point for the panel: **Apple and Google are moving towards Synapse's goals inside their own ecosystems**. So never claim "nobody does proactive suggestions". Claim cross-platform, consent-first, adaptive and student-focused guidance.

The calendar tools adapt only to what users type in. Reclaim finds time for user-entered habits like "gym 3× a week" and courted Clockwise's users after it closed ([Reclaim blog](https://reclaim.ai/blog/clockwise-shutdown-news)). Motion builds a schedule from the task list and has no free plan ([CostBench](https://costbench.com/compare/motion-vs-reclaim-ai/)). Clockwise shut down on 27 March 2026 after its team joined Salesforce ([Doodle](https://doodle.com/en/clockwise-is-shutting-down-what-to-do-next-in-2026/)). Microsoft Viva Insights builds personal insights from work email and calendar, but only inside an organisation's Microsoft 365 account ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/viva/insights/personal/overview/mya-faq)). That puts it out of reach for students on their own devices.

### Microsoft Recall shows why consent per category matters

Recall takes regular screenshots and reads them with optical character recognition (OCR, turning images of text into text) so users can search their past screen ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/privacy/privacy-and-control-over-your-recall-experience)). The 2024 preview stored its database unencrypted, a tool called "TotalRecall" showed it could be stolen, and Microsoft delayed and redesigned it as opt-in ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Recall)). Even after the redesign, testers found its filter caught labelled passwords but **saved an unlabelled list of usernames and passwords**, and card forms were captured ([PC Gamer reporting The Register](https://www.pcgamer.com/gaming-industry/new-report-alleges-microsoft-recall-is-still-screenshotting-credit-card-numbers-and-passwords/)). "Capture everything, filter afterwards" leaks credentials. Synapse's design answers this failure directly: consent per data category, skipping Android password fields (`isPassword`) and the lock screen, and never recording PINs, passwords or card numbers. Two shutdowns add a second lesson. Meta bought Limitless (formerly Rewind) in December 2025; the Rewind app shut down and recording stopped on 19 December 2025 ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2025/12/05/rewind-limitless-meta-acquisition/)). Sources even conflict on what happened to Clockwise users' data ([usecarly](https://www.usecarly.com/blog/clockwise-shut-down)). Data stranded by a vendor shutdown supports Synapse's planned export and delete-all features under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA) and the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

### Cost and availability favour a free Android-plus-web tool in Nigeria

For a typical Nigerian undergraduate on a mid-range Android phone, the free options are Digital Wellbeing, Gemini, and Samsung Routines on Galaxy phones (Samsung's Africa site documents Routines ([Samsung Africa](https://www.samsung.com/africa_en/support/mobile-devices/how-to-use-routines-on-your-samsung-galaxy-device/))). Paid tools charge in US dollars: RescueTime about **$6.50–12 a month** ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/103317/RescueTime/pricing/)), Reclaim **free to $12 a month** ([PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/reclaim)), and Motion **$19–29 a month**, depending on the source ([Techpresso](https://academy.techpresso.co/reviews/is-motion-worth-it)). Recall needs a costly Copilot+ PC ([Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/business/devices/copilot-plus-pcs)). Copilot Vision launched in the US only ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/ai/microsofts-copilot-vision-ai-helper-is-now-available-on-windows-in-the-us-160034369.html)). No source confirmed that Siri AI, Gemini screen sharing or Copilot Vision work in Nigeria as of November 2026, and naira-card payment limits were not researched.

### Peer-reviewed studies back the general argument, not product details

Peer-reviewed evidence exists for the general claims. RescueTime users failed to engage, partly for lack of context and advice ([Collins et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2686612.2686668), older than 10 years). A review of 42 digital-wellbeing apps found they mostly track and limit ([Monge Roffarello & De Russis, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300616)). Logged data beats self-report: in 404 iPhone users, self-reports were off by **110 minutes on average** and correlated only 0.3–0.4 with Screen Time logs ([Ohme et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/2050157920959106)). On the OSWorld benchmark, humans completed **72.36%** of 369 computer tasks while the best 2024 model managed **12.24%** ([Xie et al., 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/5d413e48f84dc61244b6be550f1cd8f5-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)). A 2026 paper reports agents above 85% on a verified version, but that is a **preprint** ([arXiv 2603.07978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.07978)). Either way, agents still fail on real tasks, which supports confirming every action. No peer-reviewed study of Recall, Reclaim, Motion, Viva Insights or the 2026 Gemini and Siri features was found. Cite official pages and press for those, with access dates, and label them as such.

Taken together, Synapse's six pieces are: (a) automatic routine and sequence learning, (b) across Android phone and web, (c) under consent for each data category, (d) adapting as habits change, (e) with LLM guidance aimed at students, and (f) a later screen companion that confirms every action. Each product reviewed covers at most two.

## Privacy terms, not raw quality, pick Synapse's free models

Free LLM access in late 2026 is narrower than in 2024–25. GitHub Models was fully retired on 30 July 2026 ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/github-models/use-github-models/prototyping-with-ai-models)). Hugging Face gives free users no inference credit ([HF pricing](https://huggingface.co/docs/inference-providers/pricing)). The usable free options are below. Some terms need defining first. An application programming interface (API) is the web address a program calls to use a model. A token is a word-piece, roughly three-quarters of an English word. RPM, RPD and TPM mean requests per minute, requests per day and tokens per minute. A "vision" model accepts images. "Tool calling" (or function calling) means the model can ask the app to run a named function, such as "open calendar".

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Groq `openai/gpt-oss-120b` | 30 RPM, **1,000 RPD**, 8,000 TPM, 200,000 tokens/day | Not shown (likely text-only) | Not confirmed | Not kept by default; zero data retention available |
| Cerebras free trial `gpt-oss-120b`, `qwen-3.8-27b` | 5 RPM / 1M tokens a day (gpt-oss); 300 RPM (Qwen) | Qwen: 2 images per request | Not confirmed | Terms not retrieved |
| Cloudflare Workers AI `gemma-4-26b-a4b-it` | **10,000 "neurons" a day** (about 380 short requests) | Yes (Gemma 4) | Yes (Gemma 4) | **No training, no retention** |

## Five transparent algorithms turn raw events into explainable routines

This section is for the algorithms panellist. Synapse uses five building blocks: periodic mining for "when", PrefixSpan for "what follows what", lift to filter out trivial rules, exponential decay so old habits fade, and a Hebbian-weighted graph with spreading activation for soft, context-based ranking. A sixth piece, a "settled" rule, decides when a suggestion is trusted. First, a short glossary. An **item** is one kind of event ("opened Spotify"). A **transaction** is one time window and the items in it. A **sequence** is an ordered list of items. **Support** is how often something happens. **Confidence** is how often B happens given that A happened. **Complexity** describes how running time grows with data; O(n) means doubling the data roughly doubles the work.

A warning about sources applies to this whole section. The classic algorithm papers (Apriori, FP-growth, PrefixSpan, SPADE, lift, Hebbian learning, spreading activation) are **[K]** and **older than 10 years**. They are the right original citations, but check each DOI at doi.org. Pair each one with a verified 2016+ survey where possible, as shown below.

### Periodic mining finds routines that are both frequent and regular

Periodic-frequent pattern mining finds patterns that recur at regular intervals. A pattern qualifies when its support is at least a minimum (minSup) **and** its periodicity, defined as **the largest gap between consecutive occurrences** (including the gap from the start and to the end of the data), is at most a maximum (maxPer) ([Kiran & Kitsuregawa, University of Tokyo](https://ginza.tkl.iis.u-tokyo.ac.jp/new/uploads/publication_file/file/693/Paper%2023.pdf)). The idea began with PF-growth ([Tanbeer et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-01307-2_24), [K], older than 10 years). Later variants measured regularity by average period, variance or standard deviation, and found "locally periodic" patterns that hold only during some intervals, such as term time ([Fournier-Viger, key papers](https://data-mining.philippe-fournier-viger.com/the-key-papers-about-periodic-pattern-mining/)). Verified 2016+ anchors are Fournier-Viger et al. (2019), which finds patterns periodic across many sequences ([V] [Information Sciences, DOI 10.1016/j.ins.2019.03.050](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ins.2019.03.050)); the edited book *Periodic Pattern Mining* ([V] [Springer, 2021](https://www.springer.com/gp/book/9789811639630)); and Rawassizadeh et al. (2016), who mined recurring daily behaviour from smartphone and wearable data using coarse time estimates rather than exact timestamps ([V] [LJMU repository](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/3926/)).

Synapse uses a simpler, fixed-shape version. Each event goes into a slot of (app, weekday, hour). Then two questions are asked of each slot: in how many observed weeks did this happen here (support), and when the phone was in use in this slot, how often was it this app (confidence)?

**Worked example: "opens Spotify around 08:00 on Mondays".** Eight weeks of data give eight Mondays. Spotify was opened in the 08:00–08:59 slot in weeks 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 8. **Support** is 6, and relative support is 6 ÷ 8 = **0.75**. The phone was used in that slot on 7 of the 8 Mondays, so **confidence** = 6 ÷ 7 ≈ **0.86**. A third check, temporal concentration, asks whether Spotify is tied to that hour: if it was opened 10 times on Mondays and 6 were at 08:00, that is 6 ÷ 10 = 0.6. For **periodicity**, the data runs from week 0 to week 8, and the gaps between occurrences are 1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 2 and 0 (week 8 to the end). The largest gap is **2 weeks**. With minSup = 5 and maxPer = 2, the pattern passes both tests, so it is a routine. Had the user skipped weeks 4–6, the gap would be 3, and the pattern would fail the regularity test despite decent support. That is what periodicity adds over counting.

The cost is one pass over the events with a hash map keyed by (app, weekday, hour): **O(n) time** and **O(A × 168) space** for A apps, since 7 days × 24 hours = 168 slots. General periodic itemset mining can explode exponentially with the number of distinct items. Fixing the pattern shape to "one app × one slot" avoids that. The alternatives were full PF-growth or PFPM (they find multi-item patterns but are harder to explain and unnecessary for single-app routines) and Fourier or autocorrelation analysis (they find the period automatically but need long, dense, evenly sampled signals). The chosen method is explainable ("you opened Spotify on 6 of the last 8 Mondays at 8 am"), works from a few weeks of data, and needs no training.

### PrefixSpan finds "A then B" without generating useless candidates

Two families of algorithm must be told apart. Apriori (Agrawal & Srikant, 1994, a conference paper with no DOI, [K], older than 10 years) and FP-growth ([Han, Pei & Yin, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1145/342009.335372), [K], older than 10 years) find items that happen **together**, ignoring order. Apriori relies on the downward-closure property: every subset of a frequent itemset must be frequent, so candidates with an infrequent subset are pruned. FP-growth compresses the data into a prefix tree and mines it without generating candidates. A 2016+ review of both is Luna, Fournier-Viger and Ventura ([2019, WIREs](https://doi.org/10.1002/widm.1329), [K]). GSP ([Srikant & Agrawal, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0014140), [K], older than 10 years), SPADE ([Zaki, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007652502315), [K], older than 10 years) and PrefixSpan ([Pei et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1109/TKDE.2004.77), [K], older than 10 years) find items that happen **in order**. The verified 2016+ survey covering these families is Gan et al. ([2019, ACM Transactions on Knowledge Discovery from Data](https://doi.org/10.1145/3314107), [V]).

| Method | Order matters? | How it searches | Data scans | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apriori | No | Generate candidates, then count | One per pattern length | Huge candidate sets; many scans |
| FP-growth | No | Compressed tree, recursive growth | 2 | Tree can be large; ignores order |
| GSP | Yes | Apriori-style generate and test | One per pattern length | Many candidates; slow on long sequences |
| SPADE | Yes | Joins lists of (sequence, position) pairs | About 3 | Lists and joins use memory |
| **PrefixSpan** | **Yes** | **Grows patterns inside projected suffixes** | Over shrinking projections | Recursive; time gaps need an add-on |

Synapse's question is about order ("Alarm, then Email"), so Apriori and FP-growth are the wrong tool: they would say "Alarm and Email both happen in the morning" without saying which comes first. Among ordered miners, PrefixSpan never builds candidate patterns that do not exist in the data. It only looks at what actually follows the current prefix. Each user's daily sessions are short, so these "projected" suffixes shrink fast. Pei et al. reported that PrefixSpan generally outperformed GSP and SPADE on their benchmarks; no speed-up figures were retrieved, so do not quote numbers. The "within T minutes" limit is added during projection by keeping only events at most T minutes after the prefix's last event, a constraint style described by Pei, Han and Wang ([2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10844-006-0006-z), [K], older than 10 years).

**Worked example (four made-up morning sessions; minimum support = 3).**

| Session | Sequence |
|---|---|
| S1 | Alarm → News → Email → Calendar |
| S2 | Alarm → Email → Calendar |
| S3 | News → Alarm → Email |
| S4 | Alarm → News → Calendar |

Step 1 counts single items, once per sequence: Alarm 4, News 3, Email 3, Calendar 3. All are frequent. Step 2 projects on ⟨Alarm⟩, keeping only what comes after the first Alarm: S1 → News, Email, Calendar; S2 → Email, Calendar; S3 → Email; S4 → News, Calendar. The counts are News 2, Email 3 and Calendar 3, so **⟨Alarm, Email⟩ (support 3)** and **⟨Alarm, Calendar⟩ (support 3)** are frequent. Step 3 projects on ⟨Alarm, Email⟩: S1 → Calendar, S2 → Calendar, S3 → nothing, and S4 has no Email after Alarm. Calendar reaches only 2, so the search stops there. Step 4 tries the other prefixes. After News, Email and Calendar each reach only 2; after Email, Calendar reaches only 2. The final result is the four single items plus ⟨Alarm, Email⟩:3 and ⟨Alarm, Calendar⟩:3. The rule confidence is conf(Alarm ⇒ Email) = 3 ÷ 4 = **0.75**. Now add "within 10 minutes". If Email came 25 minutes after Alarm in S2, the time filter drops it, ⟨Alarm, Email⟩ falls to support 2, and the rule is no longer reported.

On cost, Apriori and GSP can produce up to 2^m candidates for m distinct items and rescan the data at each level. PrefixSpan produces no candidates. Its work is roughly proportional to the total size of the projected suffixes, and pseudo-projection stores only pointers. Any miner's *output* can still be exponential in the worst case, so set a minimum support and a maximum pattern length (for example 3). For one user's few thousand events a week, this should run well under a second, but that is an estimate, not a benchmark.

### Lift stops Synapse from suggesting what the user does anyway

Support and confidence come from the original association-rule paper ([Agrawal, Imieliński & Swami, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1145/170035.170072), [K], older than 10 years). Lift was introduced by Brin and colleagues ([1997](https://doi.org/10.1145/253260.253325), [K], older than 10 years). Comparative studies show that high confidence misleads when the outcome B is common anyway ([Tan, Kumar & Srivastava, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4379(03)00072-3), [K], older than 10 years; [Geng & Hamilton, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1145/1132960.1132963), [K], older than 10 years). With N observed days, support(A) = count(A) ÷ N; confidence(A ⇒ B) = support(A and B) ÷ support(A); and lift(A ⇒ B) = confidence(A ⇒ B) ÷ support(B). Lift above 1 means A genuinely raises the chance of B; exactly 1 means no link; below 1 means A makes B less likely.

**Worked example.** Over 20 days, the user was "at the café in the morning" (A) on 10 days, "opened Notion" (B) on 12 days, and both on 8 days. Support of both = 8 ÷ 20 = **0.40**. Confidence = 8 ÷ 10 = **0.80**. Lift = 0.80 ÷ (12 ÷ 20) = 0.80 ÷ 0.60 = **1.33**, so the café makes Notion 33% more likely than usual. Now swap B for "opened WhatsApp", which happens on 18 of 20 days, with the same 8 joint days. Confidence is still 0.80, but lift = 0.80 ÷ 0.90 = **0.89**, below 1. The café does not drive WhatsApp use; WhatsApp happens every day regardless. Confidence alone would produce a pointless suggestion. Lift catches it. Each measure costs a few divisions, O(1) per rule.

### Exponential decay lets old habits fade without a separate change detector

When the thing being learned changes over time, machine learning calls it **concept drift**. A verified review of over 130 papers groups the responses into detecting drift, understanding it, and adapting to it ([Lu et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/TKDE.2018.2876857), [V]). An earlier survey describes "forgetting" as a core adaptation strategy, either abruptly (sliding windows) or gradually (weighting examples by age) ([Gama et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2523813), [K], just over 10 years old). Synapse uses gradual forgetting. Each event's weight halves every h days (the **half-life**): w = 2^(−Δt ÷ h), where Δt is the event's age. With h = 14 days, an event today weighs 1.0, two weeks ago 0.5, four weeks ago 0.25, and eight weeks ago 0.0625. Older precedents include time-decayed itemset counting in data streams ([Chang & Lee, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1145/956750.956807), [K], older than 10 years) and a half-life-weighted recommender ([Ding & Li, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1145/1099554.1099689), [K], older than 10 years). RecencyMiner, discussed above, is the verified smartphone-specific precedent.

**Worked example: the user stops going to the gym.** The habit is "gym on Monday at 7 am". The user went in weeks 1–6, then stopped. It is now week 10, so ages run from 0 (this week) to 9 weeks. With a half-life of 2 weeks, the six gym weeks (ages 9 down to 4) weigh 0.044 + 0.0625 + 0.088 + 0.125 + 0.177 + 0.25 = **0.746**. All ten weeks together weigh **3.308**. Decayed support is 0.746 ÷ 3.308 ≈ **0.23**, against plain support of 6 ÷ 10 = **0.60**. With a routine threshold of 0.5, plain counting would still suggest the gym. The decayed version correctly retires the suggestion four weeks after the user stopped.

The incremental form is the **exponential moving average**: S_new = α × x + (1 − α) × S_old, where x = 1 if the routine happened this period and 0 if not. To match a half-life of h periods, choose α = 1 − 0.5^(1 ÷ h); for h = 2 weeks, α ≈ 0.293. Starting at S = 0.8, three missed weeks give 0.566, then 0.400, then 0.283. The score halved after two weeks, exactly as the half-life promises. If the user restarts, two hits take 0.283 → 0.493 → 0.641, so a returning habit recovers quickly. It needs **one stored number per routine and O(1) work per update**, which suits a phone. The alternatives were a sliding window (simple, but it forgets abruptly: data counts fully or not at all), explicit drift-detection tests that trigger retraining (more complex and need tuning), and no decay (stable, but never forgets a dropped habit). No source recommends a specific half-life for phone routines. Tune h on pilot data, for example by testing 7, 14 and 28 days and keeping whichever gives the best next-week accuracy.

### A Hebbian graph with spreading activation ranks what fits the moment

Synapse's graph has nodes for apps, places, times and tasks. The update rule borrows Hebb's idea that "cells that fire together wire together" (Hebb, 1949, *The Organization of Behavior*, a book, [K], older than 10 years). Mathematical forms of that rule, including versions with decay to keep weights bounded, are reviewed by Gerstner and Kistler ([2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00422-002-0353-y), [K], older than 10 years) and Oja ([1982](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00275687), [K], older than 10 years). Each time two nodes occur together, the link grows towards 1 without passing it: w ← w + η × (1 − w), where η is the learning rate. Every day, the link shrinks slightly: w ← w × (1 − δ), where δ is the decay rate.

**Worked example: the "Gym ↔ Spotify" link**, with η = 0.2, δ = 0.05 and a starting weight of 0. Day 1 (they co-occur): 0 + 0.2 × 1 = **0.200**. Day 2: decay to 0.190, then 0.190 + 0.2 × 0.810 = **0.352**. Day 3: decay to 0.334, then 0.334 + 0.2 × 0.666 = **0.467**. Then ten days apart: 0.467 × 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 0.467 × 0.599 ≈ **0.280**. The link weakens but is not forgotten at once.

To make a suggestion, Synapse uses **spreading activation**, a theory of memory from psychology ([Collins & Loftus, 1975](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.82.6.407), [K], older than 10 years) later applied to information retrieval with decay per hop and firing thresholds to stop activation flooding the graph ([Crestani, 1997](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1006569829653), [K], older than 10 years). The current context node, say "Monday 07:00", gets activation 1.0. Links are Mon07 → Gym 0.8, Mon07 → News 0.4, and Gym → Spotify 0.9. After the first hop, activation is also multiplied by a decay factor of 0.5, and nodes below 0.1 stop spreading. Hop 1 gives Gym 1.0 × 0.8 = **0.80** and News **0.40**. Hop 2 gives Spotify 0.80 × 0.9 × 0.5 = **0.36**. The ranking is Gym, then News, then Spotify, so the guide might say "Gym time, and maybe start Spotify on the way".

Each update costs O(k²) for k events in one window, and k is small. Decay can be applied lazily from a stored "last updated" time, so it costs O(1) per link when read. With a hop limit of 2 or 3, spreading activation touches only nearby links. The graph complements the rule miners: rules give precise "A then B" facts, and the graph gives soft, context-based ranking across mixed node types. One known failure mode is over-activation, where activation spreading through a dense graph dilutes the signal ([UCLV repository](https://bida.uclv.edu.cu/handle/123456789/6854)); hop limits and thresholds control it. The alternatives were pointwise-mutual-information or lift-weighted graphs (statistically cleaner, but they need global counts and do not decay naturally), graph neural networks (powerful, but data-hungry and opaque), and random walk with restart, also called personalised PageRank (a principled cousin of spreading activation and a good "further work" item). No recent peer-reviewed survey of spreading activation for personal context graphs was found; one related source is a **preprint** ([arXiv 2512.15922](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.15922)). So present the Hebbian graph as **a design choice inspired by classic models**, not as an established benchmark method.

### Habit research sets the time scale for the "settled" rule

Synapse needs a rule for when a suggestion is "settled", meaning the system is confident the user wants it. This is different from the user's habit being automatic, and the panel may probe that distinction. Habit research sets the time scale and the tolerance for misses. A verified meta-analysis of 20 studies (2,601 adults) found **median times to habit formation of 59–66 days**, means of 106–154 days, and an individual range of 4–335 days, refuting the "21-day" myth. It also notes that 11 of the 20 studies were at high risk of bias ([Singh et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/), [V]). Lally et al. found that automaticity grows along a curve that flattens out, with a median of 66 days to plateau, and that **missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation** ([Lally et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674), [K], older than 10 years; check the figures). Habits are context-cued responses learned by repetition in stable settings ([Wood & Rünger, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417), [K]), which supports keying routines to weekday, time and place.

A proposed design rule follows: a suggestion is settled when it has been **kept on at least 8 of its last 10 offers, spread over at least 3 weeks**. The three-week floor reflects habit timescales; the 8-of-10 rule tolerates the occasional miss. A statistical version uses the **Wilson score lower bound** ([Wilson, 1927](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1927.10502953), [K], older than 10 years), which gives the lowest keep-rate that is still plausible at 95% confidence. For 9 keeps out of 10 (p = 0.9, n = 10, z = 1.96): the centre is p + z² ÷ 2n = 0.9 + 0.192 = 1.092; the margin is z × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n + z² ÷ 4n²) = 1.96 × √(0.009 + 0.0096) ≈ 0.267; the lower bound is (1.092 − 0.267) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n) = 0.825 ÷ 1.384 ≈ **0.596**. For 5 keeps out of 5, the lower bound is only about **0.566**. With a settling threshold of **0.58**, nine out of ten qualifies but five out of five does not. That is the behaviour a panel wants: a perfect but short record needs more evidence. (The research notes suggested a threshold of 0.6; the exact bound for 9/10 is 0.596, just under 0.6, so 0.58 is used here to make the example work as described.) Combine this with decay, counting keeps with recency weights, so a settled suggestion can "unsettle" if the user stops keeping it. No study links habit timescales to acceptance of system suggestions, so N and the threshold remain design parameters to tune and report.

### Deep models were rejected because Synapse must explain every suggestion

The alternatives for learning routines or predicting the next action each fail one of Synapse's requirements. Two verified surveys cover app-usage prediction methods ([Cao & Lin, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmcj.2017.01.007), [V]; [Li et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/COMST.2022.3163176), [V]). The strongest deep-learning example is **DeepApp**, which uses multi-task learning (one model trained on several related tasks at once): it adds location prediction and user identification to app prediction ([Xia et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3408325), [V]). Do not confuse it with "DeepAPP" (Shen et al., 2019), a different, reinforcement-learning system ([DOI 10.1145/3356250.3360038](https://doi.org/10.1145/3356250.3360038)). DeepApp's accuracy figures were not retrieved, so do not quote any. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, a type of recurrent neural network common in next-item prediction, come from Hochreiter and Schmidhuber ([1997](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735), [K], older than 10 years). Topic models found routines in location data ([Farrahi & Gatica-Perez, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1889681.1889684)) and activity data ([Huynh et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1409635.1409638), [K], older than 10 years). Principal component analysis (PCA) of days found "eigenbehaviors", typical day shapes ([Eagle & Pentland, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-009-0739-0), [K], older than 10 years).

A **Markov chain** is the natural baseline. It estimates P(next = B | current = A) = count(A → B) ÷ count(A). If "Alarm" was followed by Email 6 times, News 3 times and Calendar once, then P(Email | Alarm) = 0.6, P(News | Alarm) = 0.3 and P(Calendar | Alarm) = 0.1. Training is O(n) and storage is O(k²) for k apps. Its weakness is that it sees only the last step. Adding time or place to the state ("Alarm @ Monday morning") multiplies the states and the data needed.

| Approach | Data needed | Explainability | Cost | Why not Synapse's core |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Periodic + sequential mining with decay (chosen)** | Low: works after 2–4 weeks | High: "6 of the last 8 Mondays" | Cheap, incremental or nightly | — |
| Markov chain | Low to medium | High | O(n) train; O(1) lookup | Ignores time and place unless states multiply; no "within T minutes". **Use as baseline** |
| LDA topic models | Many days | Medium: topics need interpreting | Iterative, moderate | Gives "kinds of day", not next steps; topic count must be chosen |
| Clustering or PCA of days | Many days | Medium | Moderate | Describes day types; no "A then B" output |
| LSTM, attention, DeepApp | High; DeepApp pools many users | Low (black box) | GPU training; heavy on a phone | Opaque, data-hungry; pooling users conflicts with per-user data isolation |

The strongest panel argument is that Synapse's own requirements choose the method. Every suggestion must be confirmed by the user, NDPA and GDPR expect transparency, each user's data is isolated by row-level security, and each user has only weeks of data. Those requirements favour methods whose output **is** the explanation. A fair evaluation compares the chosen method with the Markov baseline and with a "most-frequent app at this hour" baseline on held-out weeks, using hit-rate at 3 (whether the true next app is among the top three suggestions). Deep models become future work only if that evaluation shows the miner's accuracy is the bottleneck.

## Conclusion

The research changes how Synapse should be pitched. The novelty is not any single algorithm. Every building block (periodic mining, PrefixSpan, decay, Hebbian graphs, LLM summaries) has precedent, and RecencyMiner already showed that recent behaviour predicts better. The novelty is **joining measurement to guidance under user control**. The literature splits into systems that learn but do not guide and systems that guide but do not learn, and their own authors name this as the gap: "actionable insights" are needed, and access to data "is not sufficient". The same logic unifies the technical choices. Explainable mining is preferred because users confirm each suggestion. Recency decay is preferred because people's behaviour drifts. No-retention models are preferred because consent per category means nothing if the data then trains someone else's model. Each decision follows from the consent-first premise, which gives the defence one coherent story rather than a list of preferences.

For the panel, each member's likely attack has a prepared answer. The **problem-statement** member gets the gap table, the Africa gap (no African country in DiversityOne; only MakOne in Uganda) and the Recall failure. The **concepts** member gets routine, concept drift, personal knowledge graph and the "settled versus automatic habit" distinction. The **algorithms** member gets five worked examples with complexities and the alternatives table. The **programming-languages** member gets one TypeScript Edge Function client that swaps OpenAI-compatible providers and keeps keys on the server. The biggest remaining risks are not conceptual. They are verification (check every [K] DOI and fill MakOne's author initials), demo reliability on free tiers, and Android background limits, which the four-year Dartmouth study flagged as the main threat to long-term sensing. Say these openly in the limitations chapter; naming them yourself is stronger than having the panel find them.
