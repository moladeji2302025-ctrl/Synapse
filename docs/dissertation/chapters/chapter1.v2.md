# CHAPTER ONE
# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background to the Study

University life runs on a set of everyday mental skills. Students must plan their day, keep track of time and remember to act at the right moment. These skills are linked to academic performance. Among 196 university students, they helped explain a large share of the differences in results [@delvalle2024]. Remembering to carry out an intention is also where people slip most often. In a diary study, forgetting to act at the right time was the commonest everyday memory error in younger adults [@haas2020]. Good time management, in turn, goes with better wellbeing and performance [@aeon2021]. When these skills fail, the effects on a student's day are immediate.

These skills weaken easily, even in healthy students. Even an unchecked smartphone nearby reduces the mental capacity a person has for other tasks [@ward2017]. Sleep matters too. Among college students, sleep quality, length and regularity explained nearly a quarter of the differences in grades [@okano2019]. Poor sleep and psychological distress have also been reported among Nigerian undergraduates [@seunfadipe2017]. Stress around examinations and deadlines affects the same skills [@shields2016]. Most students face several of these factors at once during a busy semester. As a result, planning and remembering often break down even for students with no health condition.

Health conditions weaken these skills further, from mild to severe. Anxiety is linked to poorer working memory [@moran2016], and thinking problems can follow an episode of depression [@semkovska2019]. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) affects many students, and one Ethiopian study found high ADHD symptoms in one undergraduate in four [@kassaw2025]. Students with autism show similar difficulties with daily planning and progress [@dijkhuis2020]. Illness can also leave lasting tiredness and poor concentration, as after coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [@ceban2022]. Such conditions are common. About a third of first-year students in eight countries screened positive for at least one common mental disorder [@auerbach2018].

The cost to students is real. In a study of 3,688 students, poor attention at the start of a programme predicted lower grades and more dropout [@henning2022]. Students who struggle in this way do not lack ability. Their difficulty lies in managing work, planning and keeping track of time [@weyandt2017]. Poor organisation of time also goes with a lower quality of life [@grinblat2025]. Heavier phone use goes with weaker academic results [@amez2020]. A Nigerian study across three universities examined how internet use relates to academic performance [@musa2026]. These problems therefore affect both how students feel and how well they do.

Help exists, but it is hard to reach. Group therapy with personal mentoring has improved the daily functioning of college students with attention difficulties [@anastopoulos2021]. Such programmes need trained staff, and students often wait a long time for assessment and support [@sedgwickmuller2022]. In Nigeria, stigma adds a further barrier, as mental illness is often linked to spiritual causes or moral failing [@pederson2023]. Many students also never receive a diagnosis that would open the door to such help. Most therefore cope alone, using strategies they work out for themselves, with mixed success [@canela2017]. A form of help that needs no diagnosis or clinic would reach far more of them.

The tools students can reach on their own ask for the very effort that is failing. People readily hand memory over to reminders, but every reminder must first be set up by someone [@gilbert2020]. Calendars and reminder apps hold only what users type in, and voice assistants act only when asked [@hoy2018]. A student time management app helped with planning, but it relied on tasks entered by hand [@alhasani2025]. Study reminders sent at fixed times have helped some students and hindered others [@nobbe2024]. Apps for attention difficulties also lose their users. In one study, most adults who began a seven-week app programme did not finish it [@knouse2022].

Students' phones and personal computers (PCs), meanwhile, already record how their days run. App use, place and device states such as charging leave timed traces that reveal behaviour [@caolin2017; @harari2016]. People's days also span several devices, such as a phone and a laptop [@brudy2019]. Research systems have learned routines from such data [@banovic2016; @beierle2018]. Many, however, divide the day into fixed time slots shared by all users [@sarker2018seg]. Many also weigh old behaviour as heavily as new [@sarker2019recency]. One study combined phone and computer screen time but learned no routines from it [@rooksby2016]. These systems measure behaviour, but they do not plan the day or act on it.

Tools that automate tasks also wait to be told. People can set up "if this, then that" rules, but they must write the rules themselves [@ur2016]. They also often misread how their rules behave [@brackenbury2019]. Newer agents built on large language models (LLMs) can carry out phone tasks, but only when given a command [@wen2024]. Such rules and agents fire at a set time or on a single event. They do not wait until the conditions of a routine are actually met. Recording personal activity also calls for care, because apps still track users widely [@kollnig2021]. Location data, in particular, can reveal a great deal about a person [@primault2019].

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) now make a better form of help possible. LLMs can explain information in plain language [@kasneci2023]. People expect AI to take some initiative, but they want it to act at the right moments [@meurisch2020; @zargham2022]. Any action taken on a person's behalf should also stay under their control [@amershi2019]. This project combines these strands in one system. With each student's consent, it learns their routine from their phone and computer and plans their day. It then carries out routine tasks at the moment their conditions are met, telling the student first unless told otherwise. The next section states the problem this system addresses.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

University students depend on executive function to plan their day, keep track of time and act on their intentions [@delvalle2024]. This ability slips easily [@haas2020]. Phone use, poor sleep and stress weaken it in almost every student [@ward2017; @okano2019]. Anxiety, depression, ADHD and illness weaken it further, often without any diagnosis [@auerbach2018; @henning2022]. When it slips, lectures and deadlines are missed, work is left undone and grades suffer. Help that works needs trained staff, who are scarce [@sedgwickmuller2022]. In Nigeria, stigma also stops many students from seeking such help [@pederson2023]. Most students are therefore left to manage these lapses on their own.

The tools available to them ask for the very effort that is failing. Reminders must be set up, planners must be kept, and users of such apps drop away [@gilbert2020; @knouse2022]. Automation tools make users write rules that fire at set times [@ur2016]. Research systems that learn routines rely on one device and fixed time slots [@sarker2018seg; @rooksby2016]. They also stop at measuring behaviour. What is needed is a system that learns each student's routine from their phone and computer without manual input. It should plan the student's day and carry out routine tasks when their conditions are met. It must also be shown to be accurate, usable and acceptable, and to reduce missed commitments.

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives

The aim of this project is to design and implement an AI-powered cross-device routine learning, daily planning and task automation system for executive function support in university students.

The objectives of the project are:

1. To identify the requirements of an AI-powered cross-device routine learning, daily planning and task automation system for executive function support in university students.
2. To collect activity, passive device and location data from consenting university students through their Windows PCs and Android phones.
3. To design the system's architecture, data model, and its routine-learning, daily-planning and task-automation models.
4. To implement the system as a Windows desktop application and an Android application connected through a cloud backend.
5. To evaluate the system's routine-detection and planning accuracy, task-automation performance, usability, user satisfaction, and its effect on missed commitments and everyday executive functioning.

## 1.4 Methodology

Each objective is met through a specific method that produces a specific result. The requirements come from a review of related literature and existing systems, together with a survey of students about their daily routines and needs. Activity data is then collected from students who agree to take part, through a Windows application and an Android application. The design follows from the requirements and is expressed in standard diagrams. Building happens in short development cycles, each ending in a working version that can be tested [@hoda2018]. Finally, the system is evaluated with the participating students, comparing their days before and after it begins to plan and act. Table 1.1 links each objective to its method and result.

## 1.5 Significance of the Study

This study matters first to students whose planning and memory slip, whatever the cause. The system takes over the work of planning the day, keeping time and remembering to act, without asking the student to set anything up. Its help does not depend on a diagnosis, a clinic or the student's own steady effort. Tasks happen at the moment they are due, when their conditions are met, rather than at a fixed hour. The student stays in control, because the system announces what it will do and lets the student choose how much it acts alone. It also never labels the student, which matters where mental health carries stigma. Because the system learns by itself, it keeps helping even on days when the student is too tired or stressed to plan.

The study also adds to research in this area. It shows how consent-based data from a phone and a computer can be combined to learn each student's routine. It shows how learned routines can be turned into a daily plan and into automated tasks that respect the student's control. It also offers African evidence on students' daily routines. Large datasets in this area have so far included few or no African countries [@busso2025; @kizito2025]. Finally, the study gives a worked example of collecting personal data in line with the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 [@ndpa2023]. Its methods could later be extended to other groups who struggle to manage their day.

## 1.6 Scope of the Study

This study covers a system for university students who use an Android phone and a Windows computer. With each student's consent, the system records which apps and windows are used and for how long. It also records passive events such as charging and screen use, and the student's location, turned into named places. From these records, it learns routines for each weekday, time and place, and it reads routines from documents the student uploads, such as a timetable. It also reads the student's calendar and lets planned entries take priority. It then drafts a daily plan of activities, smaller steps and timed tasks. When the student runs late or skips something, the plan adjusts for the rest of the day.

The system carries out routine tasks on either device when their conditions are met. These include scheduled messages and emails, calls it dials, calendar meetings, quiet modes for study, opening apps and alarms labelled with what comes next. The student chooses for each task whether the system suggests it, asks first or acts alone, and it announces an action before interrupting. It works offline and syncs later. It never records passwords, card details, typing or clipboard contents, and each user's data is kept separate. Users can export or delete all of their data at any time. Every action the system takes is logged, so the student can always see what it did and when.

The study leaves out iPhones, Apple computers and Linux, as well as location from internet addresses, which is too imprecise. It does not diagnose, screen for or treat any health condition, and it does not measure grades. The system makes no payments, and it never sends messages written by AI without the student's approval. The project is feasible within the time and means available. It uses free or low-cost tools, and the applications are installed directly, so no app store approval is needed. The core tasks use features that Android and Windows provide officially. A few weeks of data are enough to learn daily and weekly routines, and the work proceeds in short, tested cycles [@hoda2018].
