# LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Preamble

This chapter reviews the literature behind the system introduced in Chapter One. It begins with executive function, the mental abilities the system supports, and the many factors that weaken them in students. It then breaks the system into its basic parts and studies each one in turn. These parts include routines, the collection of activity data across devices, location, context and the methods for learning routines from data. Later sections cover daily planning, task automation, the timing of prompts, language models, offline operation, privacy and user acceptance. The chapter closes with a review of existing systems and the gap this project fills. Each section explains why its findings matter for the design of the proposed system.

## 2.2 Executive Function in University Students

### 2.2.1 Executive Function and Prospective Memory

Executive function is the set of mental processes that manage behaviour. Its core parts are working memory, which holds information in mind, inhibition, which resists distraction, and shifting, which moves attention between tasks. Planning a day, keeping track of time and remembering to act all rely on these processes. The last of these is called prospective memory, the ability to carry out an intention at the right moment. Each of these abilities is called on many times in an ordinary student's day. Among 196 university students, inhibition and shifting, with age, socio-economic status and work hours, explained 38% of performance [@delvalle2024]. Executive function therefore matters directly for students' results.

Prospective memory is also where everyday lapses most often occur. In a five-day diary study, forgetting to carry out an intention was the most frequent everyday memory error among younger adults [@haas2020]. Poor organisation of time is linked to lower quality of life in adults whose executive function is weak [@grinblat2025]. Executive deficits also mark the adults whose study, work and daily life are most impaired [@holst2020]. These difficulties are separate from intelligence. Students with attention difficulties scored lower on executive tasks, but not on intellectual ones [@weyandt2017]. The problem therefore lies in managing the day, not in ability, which is why outside support can help.

### 2.2.2 Factors that Weaken Executive Function

Executive function is not fixed, and everyday conditions weaken it in almost everyone. Even an unchecked smartphone nearby reduces the mental capacity a person has for other tasks [@ward2017]. This cost is greatest for people who depend most on their phones. Sleep matters as well. In college students tracked with wearable devices, sleep quality, duration and consistency explained nearly 25% of the variation in grades [@okano2019]. Sleep quality and psychological distress have also been studied among Nigerian undergraduates [@seunfadipe2017]. Acute stress, common around examinations and deadlines, affects the core executive functions as well [@shields2016]. Few students can avoid all of these factors during a busy semester.

Common mental health conditions weaken executive function further. Across 177 samples and over 22,000 people, anxiety was reliably linked to poorer working memory [@moran2016]. Cognitive function after a major depressive episode has also been reviewed in a meta-analysis [@semkovska2019]. Such conditions are common among students. In a survey of 13,984 first-year students in eight countries, 35% screened positive for at least one common mental disorder [@auerbach2018]. Many of these students are never assessed or diagnosed. A student may also face several of these factors at once, such as poor sleep during an anxious examination period.

More severe conditions have an even greater effect. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often lasts into adulthood and is underdiagnosed and undertreated [@kooij2019; @faraone2021]. In 3,688 post-secondary students, inattention predicted lower grades and higher dropout over time [@henning2022]. At an Ethiopian university, 25.1% of 513 undergraduates showed high ADHD symptoms [@kassaw2025]. Adults with ADHD also carry out fewer of their own intentions, which partly explains their procrastination [@altgassen2019]. In students with autism, daily executive function predicted academic progress better than autism severity [@dijkhuis2020]. Reviews also link coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) to lasting fatigue and cognitive impairment [@ceban2022].

### 2.2.3 Existing Support and Its Limits

Help for students with these difficulties exists, but it is hard to reach. Cognitive behavioural therapy with mentoring, delivered over two semesters, improved symptoms and executive function in college students with ADHD [@anastopoulos2021]. Training in organisation and study skills has also been developed for this group [@hartung2022]. Such programmes, however, need trained staff, and students often wait long for assessment and support [@sedgwickmuller2022]. In Nigeria, stigma adds a further barrier. Among Nigerian university students, mental illness was often linked to spiritual causes and moral failing [@pederson2023]. Many students therefore cope alone, using strategies they develop themselves with varying success [@canela2017; @kysow2017]. Help that depends on a diagnosis or a clinic does not reach them.

### 2.2.4 Cognitive Offloading

People often support their memory by placing information outside the mind. Cognitive offloading is the use of external aids, such as notes and alarms, to reduce a task's mental demand [@risko2016]. Setting a reminder on a phone is a common example. People rely on such reminders readily, even when their own memory would be enough [@gilbert2020]. Each reminder, however, has a cost, because someone must take the time and effort to set it up. A person whose executive function has slipped may not set up the reminder at all. The help that offloading offers is therefore lost at the very moment it is most needed.

Existing tools keep this cost, and they add the need to remember to use the tool itself. A review of a decade of apps for ADHD found that most focused on cognitive training or diagnosis [@hernandezcapistran2025]. In one study of a therapy app, only 95 of the 240 adults who started completed the seven-week assessment [@knouse2022]. Text-message reminders did not improve completion of an online programme for adults with ADHD [@nordby2022]. These findings show that reminders alone are not enough. A system that learns the routine and acts on it without being set up would remove both costs. This project describes such a system as automated cognitive offloading.

## 2.3 Routines and Habits

A routine is a pattern of actions that a person repeats in similar situations. [@banovic2016:n] describe a routine as what a person usually does in a given situation, such as checking email on reaching the office. A habit is closely related to a routine. It is behaviour that a situation prompts automatically, learned through repeating the behaviour in that situation [@gardner2022]. Habit strength also shapes how intentions turn into behaviour [@gardner2020]. Routines can therefore be learned by observing what a person repeatedly does, in which situations and at what times. A system that learns them can take over the parts of the day that the person would otherwise have to remember.

Habits take time to form, and that time varies widely between people. A review of habit-formation studies found that most habits took around two months to form [@singh2024]. In the same review, individual times ranged from a few days to almost a year. [@gardner2022:n] advise tracking habit formation through repeated measures in everyday life, rather than judging it from a single moment. These findings shape how a routine learning system should behave. It needs several weeks of data before treating a pattern as settled. It must also keep checking each routine as new data arrives, since a routine can still be forming or changing. A pattern seen for only a week or two is therefore treated as tentative.

## 2.4 Collecting Activity and Passive Data Across Devices

Smartphones can record a wide range of everyday activity through their sensors and usage logs. [@harari2016:n] describe how researchers use phones to collect behavioural data, along with the practical and ethical challenges involved. Much of this data is passive, recorded without the person doing anything, such as charging, movement and network changes. Passive sensing combined with machine learning can reveal patterns of behaviour and wellbeing [@mohr2017]. App usage is among the richest of these sources. Records of which app is opened, when and for how long support predicting future behaviour [@caolin2017; @li2022]. People also differ widely in how they use their phones [@zhao2016].

People's days increasingly span several devices, such as a phone and a laptop [@brudy2019]. Studies of computer time use show how much of a student's day happens away from the phone [@kim2016; @whittaker2016]. [@rooksby2016:n] combined screen time from phones and computers, but they did not learn routines from it. The proposed system therefore records activity on both an Android phone and a Windows computer. On Android, app usage is read through the usage statistics service once the user grants permission. On Windows, the active application and its window title are recorded. Neither source reads what the user types.

## 2.5 Location and Places

Where a person is often explains what they are doing. Phones locate themselves through satellite positioning, Wi-Fi networks and mobile masts, with lower accuracy indoors. A series of location readings over time is called a trajectory, and trajectories can be mined to find the places a person stays [@zheng2015]. Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) is a method suited to this task. It groups points that lie close together and ignores scattered ones [@schubert2017]. Knowing a person's place also improves the prediction of which app they will use next [@yu2018]. The proposed system therefore turns location readings into a small set of named places, such as the hostel and the library.

A simple example shows how DBSCAN finds a place. Suppose a student's phone records 40 location readings in the library over two hours. Suppose also that six readings are taken along the path from the hostel. DBSCAN joins any readings that lie within 50 metres of each other, provided at least five such readings are found. The 40 library readings therefore form one place, while the six scattered readings are ignored as noise. The student then names the place once. If the student later studies somewhere else, a new cluster forms there and becomes a new place. Location from internet addresses was considered and rejected, because address-based location is often wrong by large distances [@komosny2017; @gharaibeh2017].

## 2.6 Context and Context-Aware Computing

Context is any information that describes a person's situation, such as the time, the day of the week and the place [@vahdat2019]. Context-aware systems use this information to adapt what they do to that situation. On smartphones, context strongly shapes which app a person opens next. [@sarker2019eff:n] compared common machine learning methods for predicting each user's phone usage from context. Context also improves which app a personal assistant recommends to its user [@aliannejadi2021]. In the proposed system, each record carries the weekday, hour, month, place and device state, such as whether the phone is charging. A routine is then learned for a particular context, such as Monday mornings in the lecture hall.

Deep learning models can also predict app usage from context with high accuracy [@xia2020]. Such models, however, typically need large amounts of data, and their decisions are hard to explain. A routine learning system must be able to tell a student why it plans or acts as it does. It must also work with the few weeks of data that a single study period provides. The proposed system therefore uses pattern mining methods, which work well with modest data and give results that can be explained in plain words. These methods are described in the next section, together with the reasons for choosing them. Explainable results also let a student correct a routine that the system has learned wrongly.

## 2.7 Learning Routines from Data

Pattern mining is a family of methods that find regularities in large collections of records. The best-known method finds association rules, statements of the form "when A happens, B tends to happen" [@agrawal1993]. Two measures judge each rule. Support is the share of all records in which a pattern appears. Confidence is the share of records containing A that also contain B. Suppose a timetable app is opened on 6 of 8 Monday mornings. The confidence of the rule "Monday morning, then timetable app" is then 6 ÷ 8, or 0.75. Rules with high support and confidence become candidate routines for the system to act on. Weaker rules are kept as possibilities, but the system does not act on them until they strengthen.

Many routines repeat at regular intervals, such as every weekday morning or every Sunday evening. Periodic pattern mining looks for patterns that recur at such regular intervals [@zhang2022]. The proposed system applies this idea by grouping records into time slots, each made of a weekday, an hour and a place. It then counts how consistently an activity appears in each slot across the weeks. Routines are learned at several levels, from a specific weekday to weekdays in general and every day. The system uses the most specific level that holds, so a Monday routine is never confused with a Friday one. This method needs only one pass over the records.

Other routines are sequences, such as leaving a lecture hall and then turning the phone's sound back on. Sequential pattern mining finds such ordered patterns in collections of records [@gan2019]. PrefixSpan is a widely used method for this task [@pei2004]. It grows each pattern step by step from a short beginning, called a prefix. At each step, it searches only the records that can extend that prefix. For example, the prefix "leave lecture hall" grows into "leave lecture hall, then phone sound on". Because it never builds patterns that are absent from the data, it runs quickly on a phone's records. Such sequences also reveal which tasks depend on others, which the planning methods in Section 2.9 use.

Routines are not fixed, so a learning method must also let old patterns fade. [@sarker2019recency:n] showed that favouring recent behaviour predicts phone usage better than treating all history equally. The proposed system applies this principle through recency weighting. The weight of each record halves after a set period, called the half-life. With a half-life of four weeks, a record from four weeks ago counts half, and one from eight weeks ago counts a quarter. Routine changes can also be detected from daily phone use, although this work is at an early stage [@koch2020]. Fixed time slots shared by all users hide such individual differences [@sarker2018seg].

Some routines are already written down before any data is recorded, such as a lecture timetable. Large language models can extract structured information from documents like these [@xu2024]. The proposed system uses this ability to turn an uploaded timetable into routines of day, time, course and venue. Models can misread such documents, especially where table cells are merged. Each student therefore reviews the extracted entries before they become routines. Other methods could also learn routines from behaviour. Classification models predict usage from context [@sarker2019eff], and goal-driven models need far more data [@banovic2016]. Pattern mining was chosen because it is fast, explainable and suited to modest data [@sarker2021].

## 2.8 Representing What Is Learned

Learned routines must be stored in a form that the system can search and reason over. A personal knowledge graph (PKG) is one such form. It records the people, places, things and activities related to one person, and the links between them [@balog2019]. Recent surveys describe how these graphs are built, kept up to date and used for personal services [@skjaeveland2024; @chakraborty2023]. In a PKG, each item is a node, and each link between two nodes carries a label and sometimes a weight. This structure suits routines well. A routine connects an activity, a time, a place and often an earlier action, which a graph holds naturally.

The proposed system stores what it learns as such a graph. Apps, places, time slots and tasks are its nodes, and its links record which of them occur together or follow one another. Each link has a weight that grows when its two items occur together and fades when they do not. This keeps the graph in step with the user's recent behaviour, in line with the recency weighting described earlier. Knowledge graphs can also supply reliable facts to language models, which helps those models give grounded answers [@pan2024]. The system's language model draws on the graph in this way whenever it words the plan or answers a question. The graph also lets the system explain a plan by pointing to the links behind it.

## 2.9 Daily Planning, Conditions and Dependencies

A routine becomes useful to a student when it is turned into a plan for the day. Generative agents, for instance, plan a day in broad blocks first, then refine each block into hours and short steps [@park2023]. The proposed system follows the same pattern, breaking each day into activities, sub-activities and timed tasks. Fixed commitments come first, because entries in a calendar or timetable are certain while learned routines are only probable. Learned routines then fill the remaining time. The language model only words the plan. Every time in the plan comes from the student's own data, which prevents the model from inventing appointments. Because the plan is rebuilt each day, it always reflects the latest version of each routine.

Each planned task waits for its condition rather than its clock time. An event–condition–action rule states that when an event occurs and its conditions hold, an action follows [@paton1999]. Trigger-action rules in consumer automation follow the same form [@ur2016]. In the proposed system, the time is only the expected window, while the condition is the trigger. If a student is late to the library, Library mode does not start at eight o'clock. It starts when the student actually arrives. Tasks that depend on others wait in the same way. If a precondition never happens, the dependent tasks expire or move, and the student is told. This keeps automation from interrupting a student at the wrong moment.

Dependent tasks are ordered with a method called topological sorting [@kahn1962]. The tasks form a dependency graph, in which an arrow means "must happen first" and no loops are allowed. Consider three tasks: arrive at the library, turn on Library mode and open the notes app. Topological sorting places each task after the one it depends on, giving that order. A second chain, leaving the lecture hall and then turning the phone's sound back on, is ordered the same way. When something runs late, the system rebuilds the rest of the day from the current state. This step, called replanning, keeps the plan useful after the day changes. The same ordering also shows which tasks can safely move when something runs late.

## 2.10 Task Automation and User Control

Many people already automate small tasks with "if this, then that" rules. An analysis of 200,000 such rules showed the kinds of tasks people choose to automate [@ur2016]. Users, however, often misread how their own rules behave [@brackenbury2019]. Other tools learn a phone task by watching it done once [@li2017]. Earlier systems mined routines from logs and offered to automate them with the user's approval [@antila2012; @rashidi2009]. More recently, agents built on language models complete Android tasks from a typed command [@wen2024]. Others learn to operate phone apps by exploring them [@zhang2025app]. All of these tools wait to be told, and most act on a single device.

Automation must also stay under the user's control. Automation can range from merely suggesting an action to carrying it out alone [@parasuraman2000]. [@shneiderman2020:n] argues that high automation and high human control can be achieved together. Guidelines for interaction with artificial intelligence (AI) systems include making clear what the system will do and supporting easy correction [@amershi2019]. Language models also carry risks, such as acting on wrong information [@weidinger2022]. The proposed system therefore lets the student choose a level for each task: suggest, ask first or always automate. It announces an action by voice and notification before it interrupts, and it never handles payments or passwords.

## 2.11 Notifications and Their Timing

A system that prompts its users must decide what to say and when. Recommender systems suggest items or actions a user is likely to find useful, and their evaluation goes beyond accuracy alone [@zangerle2023]. Timing, however, matters as much as content. People are more open to notifications at some moments than at others, depending on what they are doing [@mehrotra2016]. Predicting opportune moments to engage phone users performs far better than a naive approach [@pielot2017]. The proposed system therefore prompts at the moment a task's condition is met, such as on arriving somewhere, rather than at a fixed clock time.

Users also want a say in how much initiative such a system takes. People's expectations of proactive AI differ, and many want to adjust how often it acts on its own [@meurisch2020]. Proactive behaviour is welcome only in suitable circumstances, and poorly judged prompts cause annoyance [@zargham2022]. Setting honest expectations helps users accept an AI that sometimes makes mistakes [@kocielnik2019]. Fixed reminders, by contrast, have shown mixed effects on students' study behaviour [@nobbe2024]. A digital nudge has also been tested as a way to reduce digital overload [@okeke2018]. The proposed system follows these lessons by keeping its prompts few, specific and well timed.

## 2.12 Intelligent Assistants and Large Language Models

Intelligent assistants are programs that help their users through conversation. Commercial voice assistants answer questions and carry out commands, but they act mainly when asked [@hoy2018]. Large language models have changed what such assistants can do. Trained on vast amounts of text, they can follow instructions and perform new tasks from a few examples in a prompt [@brown2020]. In education, they offer clear opportunities for explanation and support, alongside risks such as errors and over-reliance [@kasneci2023]. Proactive dialogue systems go further and take the initiative in a conversation [@deng2023]. The proposed system uses a language model to word its plans, briefs and replies in plain language.

Language models answer best when they are given the right information. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) first retrieves records relevant to a question, then passes them to the model to ground its answer [@lewis2020; @fan2024]. Assistants that remember their users over time also need a form of memory. Recent work surveys how such assistants store, recall and update their memories [@zhang2025]. Generative agents, for instance, retrieve memories by how recent, important and relevant they are [@park2023]. Agents that act also combine memory with planning and action [@wang2024]. The proposed system retrieves the student's routines and recent activity before answering, so its replies rest on the student's own data.

Language models also carry risks, including inaccurate answers and leaks of private information [@weidinger2022]. For this reason, the proposed system calls its model only from the server, so no secret keys ever reach the user's device. It also sends the model only the routines and summaries needed for each answer, never the raw records. The choice of model provider matters for the same reason. Providers' terms differ on whether submitted data may be stored or used to train their models. The system therefore uses a provider whose terms keep submitted data out of training. This keeps the system consistent with the consent each user has given.

## 2.13 Storing, Syncing and Acting Offline

Because the proposed system gathers data from more than one device, it must bring that data together for each user. People's interactions increasingly span several devices, which makes keeping their information consistent a challenge [@brudy2019]. A common answer is to store each user's data in one cloud database that all devices can reach. Devices, however, are not always online. Local-first software keeps a working copy of data on each device and synchronises it when a connection returns [@kleppmann2019]. The proposed system follows this pattern. Learning happens online, while each device keeps its rules and the next day's plan so that it can act offline.

Keeping each user's data separate matters as much as bringing it together. The system stores every user's records in the same database, so it must ensure that no user can read another's records. It does this through row-level security. Row-level security is a database feature that attaches an owner to every record and shows each user only their own rows. Because this rule lives inside the database itself, it holds even if the application code contains a mistake. The same rule applies to every account, including the developer's own. This protects each participant's data for the whole of the study and beyond it. Each participant can also export or delete their own rows without affecting anyone else's.

## 2.14 Privacy, Consent and Data Protection

Personal activity data is sensitive, and collecting it raises clear privacy concerns. Mobile apps track their users widely, with little change after the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) took effect [@kollnig2021]. Such practices erode people's trust in the apps they use. People who worry about privacy are less willing to complete data collection tasks on their phones [@roberts2022]. Privacy by design responds to this problem by building privacy into a system from the start, rather than adding it later [@andrade2022]. Its practices include collecting as little data as possible, telling users what is collected and giving them control over it.

Location data needs particular care. Four location points are enough to single out most people in a large mobility dataset [@demontjoye2013]. Surveys of location privacy describe protections such as coarsening locations and storing places rather than full trails [@primault2019; @jiang2021]. The proposed system follows this advice by keeping named places, not continuous paths. In Nigeria, the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 sets the rules for processing personal data [@ndpa2023]. [@mahmoud2025:n] examine how the Act applies to new technologies. Each category of data is therefore collected only after the user enables it, and users can export or delete their data at any time.

## 2.15 User Acceptance and Evaluation

A system succeeds only if its intended users accept it. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) explains acceptance through two beliefs: that a system is useful, and that it is easy to use [@davis1989]. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) adds factors such as social influence and supporting conditions [@dwivedi2019]. Both models have been applied to students. University students' adoption of ChatGPT, for example, has been explained using TAM [@zogheib2024]. In Nigeria, effort expectancy and social influence shaped computer science students' adoption of mobile learning [@makinde2027]. These models guide the questionnaire used to evaluate the proposed system.

Usability is measured with the System Usability Scale (SUS), a ten-item questionnaire that gives a score from 0 to 100 [@lewis2018]. The SUS is short, reliable and widely used, so its results are easy to compare with other systems. Everyday executive function is measured with the Adult Executive Functioning Inventory (ADEXI), a 14-item questionnaire on working memory and inhibition [@holst2018]. The ADEXI shows high internal consistency, so it suits a comparison at the start and end of the study. Where a subgroup with attention difficulties is of interest, a six-question screening scale for adult ADHD is available [@ustun2017]. It screens for symptoms but does not diagnose.

## 2.17 Review of Existing Systems

Several research systems already learn routines from phone data. Track Your Daily Routine (TYDR) is an Android app that tracks smartphone sensor and usage data to study daily routines [@beierle2018]. RecencyMiner mines each user's recent behaviour to predict their phone usage [@sarker2019recency]. [@banovic2016:n] built models of routine behaviour from the actions people were observed to take. [@koch2020:n] detected changes in daily routines from smartphone use. Each of these systems works with phone data alone. Their focus is on measuring and predicting behaviour, rather than on planning the user's day or acting on it. Table 2.2 summarises them alongside the other systems reviewed.

A second group of systems tracks time spent on computers. TimeAware showed people how they spent time on their computers, and its framing affected their productivity [@kim2016]. meTime gave people feedback on their computer use and helped them stay focused [@whittaker2016]. [@meyer2017:n] concluded that self-monitoring at work needs actionable insights, not raw data alone. [@rooksby2016:n] combined screen time from phones and computers, but only for personal tracking. These tools inform their users about past behaviour. They do not, however, learn routines from that behaviour, plan the coming day or carry out tasks.

A third group supports students and people with attention difficulties, but relies on the user's own effort. SortOut, a time management app for students, reduced stress and improved planning, but it relies on tasks entered by hand [@alhasani2025]. Digital self-control tools help people limit device use rather than manage their day [@monge2023]. Apps for ADHD mostly offer cognitive training or diagnosis [@hernandezcapistran2025]. A therapy app for adults with ADHD was usable, but most users did not complete it [@knouse2022]. Commercial voice assistants respond to commands but do not learn a user's routine on their own [@hoy2018]. Assistants that recommend apps from context focus on answering single requests [@aliannejadi2021].

A fourth group automates tasks. Trigger-action services run rules that users write themselves [@ur2016]. Another system learns a phone task from a single demonstration [@li2017]. AutoDroid completes Android tasks from a typed command using a language model [@wen2024]. AppAgent learns to operate phone apps by exploring them [@zhang2025app]. These tools act only when a user writes a rule or gives a command, and they work on one device. Regional coverage is also limited. A smartphone dataset gathered across eight countries includes no African country [@busso2025]. MakOne, collected from 72 university students in Uganda, is among the few African datasets of student device behaviour [@kizito2025].

## 2.18 Summary of the Gap

The literature shows that executive function is central to students' daily lives and easily weakened [@delvalle2024; @auerbach2018]. Support for it needs either trained staff or the student's own steady effort [@anastopoulos2021; @gilbert2020]. Some systems learn routines, but they measure rather than help [@banovic2016; @beierle2018]. Others help students, but they rely on tasks entered by hand [@alhasani2025]. Automation tools wait for rules or commands [@ur2016; @wen2024]. No reviewed system combines consent-based data from phone and computer, routines that adapt, a daily plan and tasks carried out when their conditions are met. The proposed system is designed to fill this gap, and Chapter Three describes how.
