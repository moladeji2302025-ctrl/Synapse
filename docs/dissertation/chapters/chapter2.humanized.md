# CHAPTER TWO
# LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Preamble

This chapter reviews the literature behind the system described in Chapter One. It takes the idea of an adaptive routine learning system apart and studies each of its basic parts in turn. Routines and habits come first, since they are the behaviour the system sets out to learn. Two sections on data follow: how activity data is collected, and how context shapes the behaviour it records. Next come the methods for learning routines from data, the ways of storing what is learned and the timing of suggestions. Intelligent assistants, cross-device storage, privacy and user acceptance are taken up after that. The chapter ends by explaining the technologies chosen, reviewing existing systems and stating the gap this project fills.

## 2.2 Routines and Habits

A routine is a pattern of actions that a person repeats in similar situations. Banovic et al. (2016) describe it as what a person usually does in a given situation (such as checking email on reaching the office). A habit is closely related to a routine. Habits are behaviour that a situation prompts automatically, learned by repeating that behaviour in the same situation [@gardner2022]. Seen this way, routines are chains of habits tied to times, places and earlier actions. Once formed, habits can drive behaviour even when a person intends to act differently [@gardner2020]. What people actually do may then differ from what they plan. A system that learns from real behaviour can capture the routines that planning tools miss.

Habits take time to form, and how much time varies widely between people. A review of habit-formation studies found that most habits took around two months to form [@singh2024]. Individual times in the same review ranged from a few days to almost a year. Gardner et al. (2022) advise tracking habit formation through repeated measures in everyday life; a single moment says little. Both findings bear on the design of Synapse. A routine should not be treated as settled after a few occurrences. It should be confirmed over several weeks first. The system should also keep watching for change, because habits keep forming and fading long after they first appear.

## 2.3 Collecting Activity Data from Smartphones and the Web

Through their sensors and usage logs, smartphones can record a wide range of everyday activity. Harari et al. (2016) describe how researchers use phones to collect behavioural data, along with the practical and ethical challenges involved. App usage is among the richest of these sources: which app is opened, when and for how long. Surveys show that app usage records can be used to predict future behaviour and recommend useful actions [@caolin2017; @li2022]. Large studies confirm that app usage reveals meaningful differences between users [@zhao2016]. Researchers can also draw on public app usage datasets. One of them, LSApp, holds app usage records collected from 292 users [@aliannejadi2021].

On Android, app usage can be read through the system's usage statistics service once the user grants permission. That service records which apps are used and for how long; it never reads what appears on the screen. The alternative, the accessibility service, can read screen content and is far more intrusive. Since privacy worries already make people reluctant to join phone data studies [@roberts2022], the less intrusive service is the safer choice. On the computer, activity is captured in the web browser by an extension that records the websites a user visits. Screen time combined across phones and computers gives a fuller picture than one device alone [@rooksby2016].

## 2.4 Context and Context-Aware Computing

Context is any information that describes a person's situation, such as the time, the day of the week and the place [@vahdat2019]. A context-aware system uses that information to fit what it does to the situation at hand. On smartphones, context strongly shapes which app a person opens next. Sarker et al. (2019a) compared common machine learning methods for predicting each user's phone usage from context such as time and place. Aliannejadi et al. (2021) used context to help mobile assistants pick the right app for a user's request. Time, day and place, then, are useful signals for understanding routines, and Synapse builds its routines on the same three signals.

Each record in Synapse therefore carries the weekday, hour and month, plus a coarse place where the user allows it. Routines are learned per context, so one routine might belong to weekday mornings on campus. Deep learning models can also predict app usage from context, and with high accuracy [@xia2020]. They typically need large amounts of data, though, and their decisions are hard to explain. That is a problem here, because a student using Synapse should be able to see why a suggestion was made. Synapse uses simpler pattern mining methods instead, whose results can be read and checked. The next section describes these methods and the measures they rely on.

## 2.5 Learning Routines from Data

Pattern mining is a family of methods that find regularities in large collections of records. Its best-known method finds association rules, which are statements of the form "when A happens, B tends to happen" [@agrawal1993]. Each rule is judged by two measures. Support is the share of all records in which a pattern appears, so it shows how common the pattern is. Confidence is the share of records containing A that also contain B; it shows how reliably B follows A. Suppose a timetable app was opened on 6 of the 8 Monday mornings recorded. The rule "Monday morning, then timetable app" therefore has a confidence of 6 divided by 8, or 0.75. Synapse uses both measures to score how firmly each routine is established.

Many routines repeat at regular intervals: every weekday morning, say, or every Sunday evening. Periodic pattern mining looks for patterns that recur at such intervals, and recent work refines how their regularity is measured [@zhang2022]. Synapse applies the idea by grouping records into time slots, each made of a weekday and an hour (such as Mondays at eight). For each slot it counts how consistently an app appears there over several weeks. A slot that the same app fills week after week signals a periodic routine. Because the method needs only one pass over the records, it runs quickly, even on the modest computing resources of a free cloud plan.

Other routines are sequences, such as opening email and then a calendar within a few minutes. Sequential pattern mining finds ordered patterns of this kind in collections of records [@gan2019]. PrefixSpan is a widely used method for the task [@pei2004]. It grows each pattern step by step from a short beginning, called a prefix. In the example above, the prefix would be "email", which can later grow into "email, then calendar". At each step the method searches only the parts of the data that can extend the current prefix. It never builds candidate patterns that do not occur, so it stays efficient on large collections [@gan2019]. Synapse uses PrefixSpan to find routines of the form "A, then B within a set time" in each user's records.

Routines are not fixed, so a learning method must also let old patterns fade. Sarker et al. (2019b) showed that favouring recent behaviour predicts phone usage better than treating all history equally. Synapse applies the same principle through recency weighting, in which the weight of each record halves after a set period called the half-life. With a half-life of four weeks, a record from four weeks ago counts half as much as one from today. One from eight weeks ago counts a quarter as much. A habit that stops therefore loses its score gradually. A new habit, meanwhile, gains strength week by week. The system adapts in this way as a student's routine changes across a semester.

Pattern mining is not the only way to learn routines from such data. Deep learning models predict the next app a person will open from their context [@xia2020]. Banovic et al. (2016) modelled routines as choices driven by goals, drawing on methods from planning and decision-making. Both approaches can be powerful. They need more data or more computing power, however, or they give results that are hard to explain. Pattern mining suits Synapse better, because its output is itself an explanation: "you usually open your notes app after lectures". It also works from the few weeks of records that one study period provides, and so it forms the core of the system.

## 2.6 Representing What Is Learned

Learned routines must be stored in a form that the system can search and reason over. One such form is a personal knowledge graph (PKG). A PKG records the people, places, things and activities related to one person, and the links between them [@balog2019]. Recent surveys describe how PKGs are built, kept up to date and used for personal services [@skjaeveland2024; @chakraborty2023]. Each item in a PKG is a node. Each link between two nodes carries a label, and sometimes a weight. Routines fit this structure well. A routine connects an app, a time, a place and often an earlier action, and a graph holds those connections naturally.

Synapse stores what it learns as such a graph. Its nodes are apps, places, time slots and tasks; its links record which of these occur together or follow one another. Every link carries a weight that grows when its two items occur together and fades when they do not. A link between a lecture slot and a notes app, for instance, weakens if the student stops taking notes. The graph thus stays in step with the user's recent behaviour, just as the recency weighting described earlier intends. Knowledge graphs can also supply reliable facts to language models and so help them give grounded answers [@pan2024]. Synapse's guide draws on the graph in exactly this way whenever it answers a question about the user's routines.

## 2.7 Suggestions and Their Timing

A system that guides its users must decide what to suggest and when to suggest it. Recommender systems are programs that suggest items or actions a user is likely to find useful [@zangerle2023]. Synapse's suggestions are of this kind, and they are drawn from the routines it has learned. When a suggestion arrives matters as much as what it says. People are more open to notifications at some moments than at others, depending on what they are doing [@mehrotra2016]. Predicting the opportune moments to engage phone users works far better than a naive approach [@pielot2017]. Synapse therefore times each suggestion to arrive shortly before the routine it supports usually happens, not at a fixed hour.

Users also want a say in how much initiative such a system takes. Expectations of proactive artificial intelligence (AI) differ, and many people want to adjust how often it acts on its own [@meurisch2020]. Proactive suggestions are welcome only in suitable circumstances; poorly judged ones cause annoyance [@zargham2022]. Setting honest expectations helps users accept an AI that sometimes makes mistakes [@kocielnik2019]. Fixed reminders, by contrast, have had mixed effects on students' study behaviour [@nobbe2024]. Synapse builds these lessons into its design. Every suggestion can be accepted or dismissed, and a routine becomes settled only if the user keeps accepting it.

## 2.8 Intelligent Assistants and Large Language Models

Intelligent assistants are programs that help their users through conversation. Commercial voice assistants answer questions and carry out commands, but they act mainly when asked [@hoy2018]. Large language models (LLMs) have changed what such assistants can do. Trained on vast amounts of text, LLMs can follow instructions and perform new tasks from a few examples in a prompt [@brown2020]. In education they offer real opportunities for explanation and support, alongside risks such as errors and over-reliance [@kasneci2023]. For Synapse, the useful part is their ability to explain information in plain language. A guide that talks with students about their routines needs exactly that, so an LLM serves as the guide's voice.

Language models answer best when they are given the right information. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) first retrieves records relevant to a question, then passes them to the model to ground its answer [@lewis2020; @fan2024]. An assistant that remembers its user over time also needs some form of memory. Recent work surveys how such assistants store, recall and update their memories [@zhang2025]. Generative agents are one example: they retrieve memories by how recent, important and relevant they are [@park2023]. Synapse's guide works the same way. Before answering, it retrieves the user's routines and recent activity, so its replies rest on the user's own data.

Language models also carry risks, among them inaccurate answers and leaks of private information [@weidinger2022]. Synapse calls its model only from the server for this reason, so no secret keys ever reach the user's device. The model receives only the routines and summaries needed for each answer and never the raw records. Which provider runs the model matters too, for the same reason. Providers' terms differ on whether submitted data may be stored, or used to train their models. Synapse uses a provider whose terms keep submitted data out of storage and training alike. The guide therefore stays within the consent each user has given, even once a summary has left the server.

## 2.9 Storing and Syncing Data Across Devices

Synapse gathers data from more than one device, so it must bring that data together for each user. People's interactions increasingly span several devices, and keeping their information consistent across them is a challenge [@brudy2019]. A common answer is to store each user's data in one cloud database that all devices can reach. The catch is that devices are not always online. Local-first software keeps a working copy of data on each device and synchronises it when a connection returns [@kleppmann2019]. Synapse's collectors follow that pattern. Each device holds its records in a queue and sends them to the database once it is back online.

Keeping each user's data separate matters as much as bringing it together. Every user's records sit in the same database, so Synapse has to make sure that no user can read another user's records. It does this with row-level security, a database feature that attaches an owner to every record and shows each user only their own rows. The rule lives inside the database itself. It therefore holds even if the application code contains a mistake, which a check written into the app would not. The same rule applies to every account, the developer's own included; no account is given a way around it. Each participant's data is protected in this way for the whole of the study, and after it ends.

## 2.10 Privacy, Consent and Data Protection

Personal activity data is sensitive, and collecting it raises clear privacy concerns. Mobile apps track their users widely, and little changed after the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) took effect [@kollnig2021]. Practices like these erode people's trust in the apps they use. People who worry about privacy are less willing to complete data collection tasks on their phones [@roberts2022]. Privacy by design answers the problem by building privacy into a system from the start, not bolting it on afterwards [@andrade2022]. In practice that means collecting as little data as possible, telling users what is collected and giving them control over it.

In Nigeria, the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 sets the rules for processing personal data [@ndpa2023]. The Act requires a lawful basis for processing, such as consent, and it protects people's rights over their data. Mahmoud et al. (2025) examine how it applies to new technologies and stress the need to balance innovation with privacy. Synapse is designed to meet these rules from the outset. Each category of data is collected only after the user enables it. Passwords, personal identification numbers (PINs) and card details are never recorded, whatever the user enables. Users can also see, export and delete their data at any time, so control stays with them.

## 2.11 User Acceptance and Evaluation

A system succeeds only if its intended users accept it. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) explains acceptance through two beliefs: that a system is useful, and that it is easy to use [@davis1989]. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) adds further factors, among them social influence and supporting conditions [@dwivedi2019]. Both models have been applied to students. TAM has been used to explain university students' adoption of ChatGPT [@zogheib2024]. In Nigeria, effort expectancy and social influence shaped computer science students' adoption of mobile learning [@makinde2027]. These models guide the questionnaire used to evaluate Synapse.

Usability is measured with the System Usability Scale (SUS), a ten-item questionnaire that gives a score from 0 to 100 [@lewis2018]. The SUS is short, reliable and widely used, which makes its results easy to compare with those of other systems. Suggestion quality is a separate matter from usability. Frameworks for evaluating recommender systems stress measuring both accuracy and the experience of the user [@zangerle2023]. Synapse is therefore evaluated in several ways. Detected routines are compared with the routines participants confirm. Flexibility is tested by how quickly routines adapt when behaviour changes. Participants also rate the system's usefulness and ease of use, and their overall satisfaction with it.

## 2.12 Technologies Used and Why They Were Chosen

Table 2.1 summarises the technologies in Synapse and the reason each was chosen. The Android app is written in Kotlin with the Jetpack Compose interface toolkit. Góis Mateus and Martinez (2019) found that adopting Kotlin in Android apps was associated with fewer code smells (patterns in code that signal poor design). The web app uses React, one of the three web frameworks whose performance Bielak et al. (2022) compared. React builds an interface from small reusable parts called components. For the backend, Supabase provides a PostgreSQL database, sign-in and server functions in one free service. Development follows an agile approach of short cycles, each of which produces working results [@hoda2018].

## 2.13 Review of Existing Systems

Several research systems already learn routines from phone data. Track Your Daily Routine (TYDR) is an Android app that tracks smartphone sensor and usage data to study daily routines [@beierle2018]. RecencyMiner mines each user's recent behaviour to predict their phone usage [@sarker2019recency]. Banovic et al. (2016) built models of routine behaviour from the actions people were observed to take. Koch et al. (2020) detected changes in daily routines from the smartphone use of 60 users. All four work with phone data alone. They are built to measure and predict behaviour, not to guide the user during the day. Table 2.2 summarises them alongside the other systems reviewed.

A second group of systems tracks time spent on computers. TimeAware showed people how they spent time on their computers, and the way it framed that information affected their productivity [@kim2016]. meTime gave people feedback on their computer use and helped them stay focused [@whittaker2016]. Meyer et al. (2017) concluded that self-monitoring at work needs actionable insights, not raw data alone. Rooksby et al. (2016) combined screen time from phones and computers, though only for personal tracking. All of these tools tell their users about past behaviour. None learns routines from that behaviour or suggests what to do next.

A third group guides users without learning from their behaviour. SortOut, a time management app for students, reduced stress and improved planning, yet it relies on tasks entered by hand [@alhasani2025]. Good Vibrations used phone vibrations to nudge people towards less phone use; the effect faded once the nudges stopped [@okeke2018]. Digital self-control tools help people limit device use, but they do not help them understand their routines [@monge2023]. Commercial voice assistants respond to commands and do not learn a user's routine on their own [@hoy2018]. Research assistants that recommend apps from context, meanwhile, focus on answering single requests [@aliannejadi2021].

Coverage of different regions in this field is also limited. A recent smartphone dataset gathered across eight countries includes no African country [@busso2025]. One of the few African datasets of student device behaviour is MakOne, collected from 72 university students in Uganda [@kizito2025]. Studies of Nigerian students' device use have mainly described usage patterns; they have not learned routines from them [@musa2026]. This review found no routine learning system built for Nigerian students. Synapse responds to that gap by focusing on students of a Nigerian university. The system is designed around their devices, their consent and the shape of their academic week.

## 2.14 Summary of the Gap

Each part of Synapse exists somewhere in the literature, but no single system brings them together. Some systems learn routines, yet they measure behaviour instead of guiding it [@banovic2016; @beierle2018]. Others guide users without learning from their behaviour [@alhasani2025]. Routine learning usually draws on one device, and it treats old behaviour like new [@sarker2018seg; @sarker2019recency]. Privacy concerns, meanwhile, make consent essential for any such system [@roberts2022]. None of the systems reviewed combines consent-based phone and web data, routines that adapt over time and guidance from a language model for students. That combination is the gap Synapse is designed to fill. Chapter Three describes how.
