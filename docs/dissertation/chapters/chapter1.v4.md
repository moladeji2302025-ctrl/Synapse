# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background to the Study

University life runs on a few everyday mental skills: planning the day, keeping track of time and remembering to act at the right moment. They matter. Among 196 university students, executive abilities such as these accounted for much of the difference in academic performance, alongside age and work hours [@delvalle2024]. Forgetting is the usual failure. In one diary study, forgetting to act at the right time was the commonest everyday memory error in younger adults [@haas2020]. Good time management goes with better wellbeing and performance [@aeon2021], and when it fails, a planned lecture, deadline or task simply does not happen.

The skills are fragile, even in healthy students. A smartphone lying nearby, unchecked, still cuts the mental capacity a person has left for other tasks [@ward2017]. Sleep matters too. Among college students, sleep quality, length and regularity explained nearly a quarter of the variation in grades, and it was the month and week before a test that counted, not the night before [@okano2019]. Sleep quality and psychological distress have also been studied among Nigerian undergraduates [@seunfadipe2017]. Stress around examinations and deadlines affects the same skills [@shields2016]. Few students escape all of these. Planning and remembering therefore break down often, and not only for students who live with a health condition.

Health conditions weaken the skills further, from mild to severe. Anxiety goes with poorer working memory [@moran2016]. Thinking can also suffer after an episode of depression [@semkovska2019]. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is common among students, and one Ethiopian study found high ADHD symptoms in one undergraduate in four [@kassaw2025]. Students with autism show similar difficulty with daily planning and academic progress [@dijkhuis2020]. Illness can leave lasting tiredness and poor concentration, as after coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [@ceban2022]. None of this is rare: about a third of first-year students in eight countries screened positive for at least one common mental disorder [@auerbach2018].

The cost to students is real. Among 3,688 students followed over time, poor attention at the start of a programme predicted lower grades and more dropout [@henning2022]. These students do not lack ability. Their difficulty lies in managing work, planning and keeping track of time [@weyandt2017]. Poor organisation of time also goes with a lower quality of life [@grinblat2025]. Heavier phone use goes with weaker academic results [@amez2020], and a Nigerian study across three universities examined how internet use relates to academic performance [@musa2026]. The problems touch how students feel and how well they do. They also feed one another.

Help exists, but it is hard to reach. Group therapy with personal mentoring improved the daily functioning of college students with attention difficulties [@anastopoulos2021]. Programmes like this need trained staff, and students often wait a long time for assessment and support [@sedgwickmuller2022]. In Nigeria, stigma adds a second barrier, because mental illness is often put down to spiritual causes or moral failing [@pederson2023]. Many students never receive the diagnosis that would open the door. Most cope alone, with strategies they work out for themselves and with mixed success [@canela2017]. Help that needed no diagnosis and no clinic would reach far more of them.

The tools students can reach alone ask for the effort that is failing. People readily hand memory over to reminders, but someone must first set each one up [@gilbert2020]. Calendars and reminder apps hold only what users type in. Voice assistants answer only when asked [@hoy2018]. A student time-management app helped with planning, yet every task had to be entered by hand [@alhasani2025]. Study reminders sent at fixed times helped some students and hindered others [@nobbe2024]. Apps for attention difficulties lose users too: in one study, most adults who began a seven-week app programme did not finish it [@knouse2022].

Students' phones and personal computers (PCs), meanwhile, already record how their days run. App use, place and device states such as charging leave timed traces that reveal behaviour [@caolin2017; @harari2016]. A day also spans several devices, such as a phone and a laptop [@brudy2019]. Research systems have learned routines from such data [@banovic2016; @beierle2018]. Many split the day into fixed time slots that are the same for every user [@sarker2018seg]. Many weigh old behaviour as heavily as new [@sarker2019recency]. One study combined phone and computer screen time but learned no routines from it [@rooksby2016]. These systems measure behaviour. They neither plan the day nor act on it.

Automation tools also wait to be told. People can set up "if this, then that" rules, but they must write them themselves [@ur2016], and they often misread how the rules behave [@brackenbury2019]. Newer agents built on large language models (LLMs) can carry out phone tasks, but only on command [@wen2024]. Rules and agents like these fire at a set time or on a single event. They do not wait until the conditions of a routine are really met. Recording personal activity also calls for care, because apps still track users widely [@kollnig2021]. Location data in particular can reveal a great deal about a person [@primault2019].

Recent progress in artificial intelligence (AI) now makes a better form of help possible. LLMs can explain information in plain language [@kasneci2023]. People expect AI to take some initiative, provided it acts at the right moments [@meurisch2020; @zargham2022], and any action taken for a person should stay under their control [@amershi2019]. This project brings these strands together in one system. With each student's consent, it learns their routine from their phone and computer and plans their day. It then carries out routine tasks at the moment their conditions are met, telling the student first unless told otherwise. The next section states the problem it addresses.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

University students depend on executive function to plan their day, keep track of time and act on their intentions [@delvalle2024]. It slips easily [@haas2020]. Phone use, poor sleep and stress weaken it in almost every student [@ward2017; @okano2019]. Anxiety, depression, ADHD and illness weaken it further, often with no diagnosis [@auerbach2018; @henning2022]. When it slips, lectures and deadlines are missed, work is left undone and grades suffer. Help that works needs trained staff, and they are scarce [@sedgwickmuller2022]. In Nigeria, stigma also stops many students from asking for it [@pederson2023]. Most are left to manage the lapses alone.

The tools open to them ask for the very effort that is failing. Reminders must be set up, planners must be kept, and users of such apps drop away [@gilbert2020; @knouse2022]. Automation tools make users write rules that fire at set times [@ur2016]. Research systems that learn routines rely on one device and fixed time slots, and they stop at measuring behaviour [@sarker2018seg; @rooksby2016]. What is needed is a system that learns each student's routine from phone and computer without manual input. It should plan the day and carry out routine tasks when their conditions are met. It must also be shown to be accurate, usable and acceptable, and to reduce missed commitments.

## 1.3 Aim and Objectives

The aim of this project is to design and implement an AI-powered cross-device routine learning, daily planning and task automation system for executive function support in university students.

The objectives of the project are:

1. To identify the requirements of an AI-powered cross-device routine learning, daily planning and task automation system for executive function support in university students.
2. To collect activity, passive device and location data from consenting university students through their Windows PCs and Android phones.
3. To design the system's architecture, data model, and its routine-learning, daily-planning and task-automation models.
4. To implement the system as a Windows desktop application and an Android application connected through a cloud backend.
5. To evaluate the system's routine-detection and planning accuracy, task-automation performance, usability, user satisfaction, and its effect on missed commitments and everyday executive functioning.

## 1.4 Methodology

Each objective is met by a specific method that yields a specific result. The requirements come from a review of related literature and existing systems, together with a survey of students about their routines and needs. Activity data is then collected from students who agree to take part, through a Windows application and an Android application. The design follows from the requirements and is drawn in standard diagrams. Building proceeds in short cycles, and each cycle ends in a working version that can be tested [@hoda2018]. Finally, the system is evaluated with the participating students. Their days are compared before and after it begins to plan and act. Table 1.1 links each objective to its method and result.

## 1.5 Significance of the Study

The study matters first to students whose planning and memory slip, whatever the cause. The system takes over three jobs: planning the day, keeping time and remembering to act. Nothing has to be set up. Its help does not depend on a diagnosis, a clinic or the student's own steady effort, so it keeps working on days when the student is too tired or stressed to plan. Tasks happen when their conditions are met, not at a fixed hour. The student stays in control, because the system announces what it will do and lets the student choose how much it acts alone. It never labels the student either, which matters where mental health carries stigma.

The study also adds to research. It shows how consented data from a phone and a computer can be combined to learn each student's routine. It shows how learned routines can become a daily plan and automated tasks that respect the student's control. It brings African evidence to a field where large datasets have included few or no African countries [@busso2025; @kizito2025]. And it gives a worked example of collecting personal data in line with the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 [@ndpa2023]. The same methods could later serve other groups who find it hard to manage their day.

## 1.6 Scope of the Study

The study covers a system for university students who use an Android phone and a Windows computer. With each student's consent, the system records which apps and windows are used and for how long. It also records passive events, such as charging and screen use, and the student's location, turned into named places. From these records it learns routines for each weekday, time and place. It reads routines from documents the student uploads, such as a timetable, and it reads the student's calendar, letting planned entries take priority. It then drafts a daily plan of activities, smaller steps and timed tasks. When the student runs late or skips something, the plan adjusts for the rest of the day.

The system carries out routine tasks on either device once their conditions are met. These include scheduled messages and emails, calls it dials, calendar meetings, quiet modes for study, opening apps and alarms labelled with what comes next. For each task the student chooses whether the system suggests, asks first or acts alone. It announces an action before interrupting. It works offline and syncs later. It never records passwords, card details, typing or clipboard contents, and each user's data is kept separate. Users can export or delete all of their data at any time. Every action is logged, so the student can always see what the system did and when.

The study leaves out iPhones, Apple computers and Linux. It also leaves out location from internet addresses, which is too imprecise. It does not diagnose, screen for or treat any health condition, and it does not measure grades. The system makes no payments, and it never sends AI-written messages without the student's approval. The project is feasible with the time and means available. It uses free or low-cost tools, and the applications are installed directly, so no app store approval is needed. The core tasks rely on features that Android and Windows provide officially. A few weeks of data are enough to learn daily and weekly routines, and the work proceeds in short, tested cycles [@hoda2018].
